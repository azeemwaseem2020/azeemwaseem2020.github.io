Newton's second law and net force

Newton's second law connects net force, mass and acceleration. The word net is important: when several forces act on an object, their vector sum determines the acceleration.

Worked example

A 20 kg object with a net force of 60 N has an acceleration of 3 m/s². If opposing forces are present, they must be included before calculating the net force.

Free-body thinking

For more realistic problems, identify all relevant forces first, choose consistent directions and then sum their components.

Newton's-law FAQ

Can a moving object have zero net force?

Yes. With zero net force, acceleration is zero, so an object can continue moving at constant velocity in an ideal inertial frame.

Using the result in a real problem

The calculator returns F = m × a, where mass is measured in kilograms and acceleration in metres per second squared, giving force in newtons. The acceleration should represent the object's net acceleration in the direction being analysed.

Check the direction of forces

If friction, tension, gravity, a normal reaction or another applied force is involved, do not automatically treat one force as the net force. Draw a simple free-body diagram, choose a positive direction and add the force components with their signs.

Example with opposing forces

Suppose a 10 kg cart has 50 N pushing forward and 20 N of resistance. The net force is 30 N, so its acceleration is 3 m/s². Entering 50 N as the net force would incorrectly ignore the opposing force.

Newton’s Second Law Calculator: Net Force and Acceleration

Newton’s second law connects net force, mass and acceleration. It explains how an object’s motion changes when the forces acting on it do not balance. The key word is net: pushes, pulls, friction and other forces must be combined with their directions before calculating acceleration. This calculator is useful for mechanics problems and checking unit conversions.

Formula and units

The law is Fnet = m × a. Rearranging gives a = Fnet ÷ m and m = Fnet ÷ a, when the relevant divisor is not zero. In SI units, force is measured in newtons (N), mass in kilograms (kg), and acceleration in metres per second squared (m/s²). One newton equals one kg·m/s². Convert grams to kilograms or other force units before calculating unless the tool explicitly supports those units.

How to use the calculator

  1. Identify which of force, mass or acceleration is unknown.
  2. Enter the other two quantities in compatible SI units.
  3. Calculate and read the result with its proper unit.
  4. For a real object, determine net force by accounting for opposing forces and directions.

Worked examples

Example 1: A 5 kg trolley experiences a net force of 20 N. Its acceleration is 20 ÷ 5 = 4 m/s². Example 2: If a 12 kg object accelerates at 2.5 m/s², its net force is 12 × 2.5 = 30 N. Example 3: A cart has 50 N pushing forward and 20 N of resistance. Net force is 30 N; for a 10 kg cart, acceleration is 30 ÷ 10 = 3 m/s². Using 50 N alone would ignore resistance.

Assumptions and common mistakes

The simple equation applies to net force on a body of constant mass in a chosen reference frame. It does not mean every individual force independently equals mass times acceleration. Choose a positive direction and assign signs consistently when forces oppose one another. Mass is not weight: weight is a gravitational force, approximately m × g near Earth’s surface. A stationary object can still have forces acting on it if those forces balance. More complex motion, friction or changing mass may require a fuller model.

Frequently asked questions

What if net force is zero?

Acceleration is zero. The object remains at rest or continues at constant velocity if no other unmodelled effects act.

Is mass measured in newtons?

No. Mass is measured in kilograms; newtons measure force or weight.

Why use net force?

All forces contribute to the overall force after their directions are taken into account.
