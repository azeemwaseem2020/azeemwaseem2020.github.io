Newton's second law: F = ma

Newton's second law relates net force, mass and acceleration: F = m × a. In SI units, force is measured in newtons, mass in kilograms and acceleration in metres per second squared. The important word is net: if several forces act on an object, their vector sum determines acceleration.

Worked example

A 10 kg object experiencing a net acceleration of 2 m/s² has a net force of 10 × 2 = 20 N. If the force is known and mass is known, the same relationship can be rearranged to calculate acceleration or mass.

Practical example

For machinery, vehicles or laboratory experiments, identify the net force rather than one individual force. Friction, gravity and applied force may all contribute to the final result.

FAQs

What is a newton? One kilogram metre per second squared. Does F = ma use net force? Yes. Can mass be zero? Not for an ordinary physical object in this model.

Using F = ma in real problems

Newton's second law applies to the net external force on an object. If a 10 kg trolley is pushed with 30 N while friction opposes it with 10 N, the net force is 20 N and acceleration is 20/10 = 2 m/s². This illustrates why adding only the applied force can give the wrong answer.

For two- or three-dimensional problems, force is a vector and direction matters. The calculator's simple relationship is most useful when the relevant quantities have already been reduced to a one-dimensional net-force problem. Keep kilograms, metres and seconds consistent for SI units.

Apply Newton's second law correctly

Newton's second law states that the net force on an object equals its mass multiplied by its acceleration: F = m × a. In SI units, mass is measured in kilograms, acceleration in metres per second squared, and force in newtons. One newton is the force required to accelerate one kilogram at 1 m/s².

Worked example

If a 12 kg trolley has a net acceleration of 2.5 m/s², the net force is 12 × 2.5 = 30 N. This is the net force, not necessarily the force applied by one person; friction and other forces may also act on the trolley.

Check assumptions and units

  • Use mass in kilograms and acceleration in m/s² for a result in newtons.
  • Use the net force after accounting for all relevant forces, not just one force in isolation.
  • Acceleration has direction; a negative value can indicate the chosen coordinate direction rather than an invalid calculation.
  • For changing mass systems or relativistic speeds, the simple introductory form may not be sufficient.

Frequently asked questions

What happens if acceleration is zero? The net force is zero in this model, although individual forces may balance one another.

Can I enter weight in kilograms as force? Kilograms measure mass. Weight is a force measured in newtons and depends on gravitational acceleration.

Does a larger force always mean a larger acceleration? For the same mass, a larger net force produces a proportionally larger acceleration under Newton's second law.
