Probability Calculator

Calculate the probability and percentage chance of a simple event from favourable and total equally likely outcomes.

Calculate simple probability

Simple probability formula

P(event) = favourable outcomes ÷ total possible outcomes

To express probability as a percentage, multiply the probability by 100.

Worked example

For one favourable outcome out of six equally likely outcomes, P = 1 ÷ 6 = 0.1667, or about 16.67%.

How to use this probability calculator

  1. Enter the number of favourable outcomes.
  2. Enter the total number of possible outcomes.
  3. Select Calculate Probability.
  4. Review the decimal probability and percentage chance.

Probability and percentage

Probability is commonly written from 0 to 1, while the equivalent percentage runs from 0% to 100%. For example, 0.25 is the same as 25%.

Probability versus odds

Probability describes the chance of an event occurring. Odds compare favourable outcomes with unfavourable outcomes. They are related but are not the same measurement.

Common uses

Simple probability is useful for basic statistics, classroom exercises, equally likely games of chance and quick checks where favourable and total outcomes are already known.

Important assumption

This calculator assumes the outcomes are equally likely and that the favourable outcomes are counted correctly. It does not calculate conditional probability, combinations, permutations or dependent events.

Probability interpretation

Probability describes uncertainty using values from 0 to 1, or equivalently 0% to 100%. The calculation only has meaning when the event and underlying assumptions are defined clearly.

Worked check

If 3 outcomes out of 10 equally likely outcomes satisfy an event, its probability is 3/10 or 0.30. If outcomes are not equally likely, simple counting may not be appropriate.

Independence matters

Multiplying probabilities for separate events requires the appropriate independence or conditional-probability assumptions. Do not automatically treat related events as independent.

Probability Calculator: Formula, Outcomes and Worked Examples

Probability describes how likely an event is, using values from 0 to 1 or from 0% to 100%. A value of 0 represents an impossible event within the model, while 1 represents a certain event. A probability estimate is only as sound as the assumptions about possible outcomes and how likely they are.

Basic formula

Worked examples

A fair six-sided die has three even outcomes: 2, 4 and 6. The probability of rolling an even number is 3 ÷ 6 = 0.5 or 50%. For two independent fair coin tosses, the probability of two heads is 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4, or 25%. If events are not independent, multiplying their separate probabilities may give the wrong result.

How to use the calculator

  1. Define the event clearly and identify the total outcome count or required probability inputs.
  2. Enter favourable and total outcomes when using the equally-likely model.
  3. Check that favourable outcomes cannot exceed total outcomes and that the total is positive.
  4. Calculate and read the result as a fraction, decimal or percentage as displayed.
  5. State assumptions such as fairness, independence or replacement when sharing the answer.

Features and applications

The calculator can support school mathematics, basic risk comparisons, games and introductory statistics. It makes fractions and percentages easier to compare and helps users check complementary probabilities. In sampling problems, the way items are selected matters: drawing with replacement can preserve probabilities, while drawing without replacement changes the available outcomes after each draw.

Interpretation and limitations

Probability is not a promise about one individual outcome. A 25% chance does not mean an event must occur exactly once in every four attempts; it describes the model's chance for each relevant trial. Long-run frequencies may approach the probability under suitable repeated, stable conditions, but short sequences can vary. Historical frequency is also not automatically a reliable forecast if the process changes.

Some problems have outcomes that are not equally likely, and they require weighted probabilities or data rather than a simple favourable-count ratio. Avoid confusing probability with odds: probability compares favourable outcomes with all outcomes, while odds compare favourable with unfavourable outcomes. For medical, financial or safety decisions, a simple probability calculation may omit important context and uncertainty.

Frequently asked questions

Can probability be greater than 100%?

No. A valid probability is between 0 and 1, inclusive, or 0% to 100%.

What is the complement of a 30% chance?

The probability that the event does not occur is 70%, under the same model.

Are odds and probability the same?

No. Probability uses favourable outcomes over all outcomes; odds compare favourable with unfavourable outcomes.

Read probability as a proportion

Probability ranges from 0 to 1, equivalent to 0% to 100%. A probability of 0.25 means a 25% chance under the stated model; it does not guarantee that an event will occur once in every four trials. Long-run frequency and the probability of a single outcome are related but not identical.

Use the right rule for the event

For mutually exclusive outcomes, probabilities can be added to find the chance of either outcome. For independent events, the probability that both occur is the product of their probabilities. If events are dependent, the second probability must be conditioned on the first event rather than multiplied as if nothing changed.

Example: drawing from a deck

In a standard 52-card deck, the probability of drawing an ace on one draw is 4/52, or about 7.69%. If the card is not replaced, the deck composition changes, so the probability on a second draw is conditional on what happened first. Replacement and no-replacement questions require different models.

Assumptions matter

A numerical result is only as reliable as the event definition and assumptions. Clearly define the sample space, check whether outcomes are equally likely, and distinguish observed frequency from a theoretical probability. This calculator does not establish that a real-world event is random or independent.

Define the event before calculating probability

Probability describes uncertainty on a scale from 0 to 1, where 0 means impossible under the model and 1 means certain. The simple formula favourable outcomes ÷ total outcomes applies only when the listed outcomes are equally likely and the sample space is complete.

Worked example: a fair six-sided die

The probability of rolling an even number is 3/6 = 1/2 = 50%, because the favourable outcomes are 2, 4 and 6. The probability of not rolling an even number is the complement, 1 − 0.5 = 0.5.

Independence and changing sample spaces

For independent events A and B, P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). In a draw without replacement, the second draw's probabilities change because the sample space changes. Do not multiply probabilities as if events were independent unless assumptions support it. Observed frequency is not automatically theoretical probability.

Frequently asked questions

Can probability exceed 100%?

No. A valid probability is between 0% and 100%, inclusive.

Are odds the same as probability?

No. Probability compares favourable outcomes with all outcomes; odds compare favourable outcomes with unfavourable outcomes.

When can I use favourable outcomes divided by total outcomes?

When outcomes are equally likely and the sample space is correctly defined. Otherwise, a suitable model or data-based estimate is needed.
