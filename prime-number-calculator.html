Prime number test: method and examples

A prime number is an integer greater than 1 with exactly two positive divisors: 1 and itself. A composite number has additional positive divisors. The calculator tests whether the entered integer is prime; it does not factor the number or list all divisors.

How the test works

For an integer n greater than 1, the script checks candidate divisors from 2 up to the square root of n. If any candidate divides n evenly, n is composite. If none does, n is prime. Checking beyond the square root is unnecessary because any factor larger than √n must pair with a factor smaller than √n.

Examples

17 is prime because no integer from 2 through 4 divides it evenly. 21 is not prime because 3 × 7 = 21. The numbers 0 and 1 are not prime, and negative integers are not prime under the usual elementary definition.

How to use the calculator

  4. If the input is rejected, check that it is an integer of at least 2; the current tool does not return a result for zero, one or negative values.

Applications and limitations

Prime numbers are used in number theory, mathematics education and some areas of computing. This simple trial-division method is suitable for ordinary educational examples but becomes slower for very large integers. A prime test result alone does not provide the prime factorization of a composite number, and this tool should not be used as a cryptographic security primitive.

Frequently asked questions

Is 1 a prime number?

No. A prime number must have exactly two distinct positive divisors; 1 has only one.

Why only check up to the square root?

Factors occur in pairs. If both factors were greater than the square root, their product would be greater than n, so at least one factor of a composite number must be at or below √n.

Is every odd number prime?

No. Many odd numbers are composite, such as 9, 15 and 21. Oddness rules out divisibility by 2, but other divisors may still exist.

Prime Number Calculator: Definition, Divisibility and Examples

A prime number is a whole number greater than 1 with exactly two positive divisors: 1 and itself. A composite number has more than two positive divisors. The number 1 is neither prime nor composite, which is an important edge case for any prime-number test.

How primality is checked

For an integer n greater than 1, a straightforward test checks whether any integer from 2 through the square root of n divides it evenly. If a divisor is found, n is composite; if none is found, n is prime. Only checking up to √n is enough because if n = a × b and both factors were greater than √n, their product would exceed n.

Worked examples

29 is prime: its square root is about 5.39, so test divisors 2, 3 and 5; none divides 29 evenly. By contrast, 35 is composite because 35 ÷ 5 = 7 with no remainder. The number 2 is the only even prime; any even integer greater than 2 is divisible by 2 and therefore composite.

Limitations and common mistakes

Do not classify 1 as prime, and do not treat zero or negative integers as prime under the standard positive-integer definition. Decimal values are not prime numbers in this sense. A simple trial-division approach can become slow for extremely large inputs; a fast result for a small number should not be taken as evidence that the page can test cryptographic-scale integers safely or efficiently.

Prime status is a mathematical property, not a measure of randomness or security by itself. A number being prime does not mean it is suitable for cryptographic use, and this calculator does not generate or certify secure keys. Use the result for education and general arithmetic unless the implementation explicitly documents stronger capabilities.

Is 1 a prime number?

No. A prime number has exactly two distinct positive divisors, while 1 has only one.

Why is 2 prime?

Its only positive divisors are 1 and 2, making it the only even prime.

Why do we only test divisors up to the square root?

Any composite number has at least one factor no greater than its square root.
