Prime numbers and factor checking

A prime number is an integer greater than 1 with exactly two positive divisors: 1 and itself. Numbers such as 2, 3, 5, 7 and 11 are prime. A number greater than 1 with additional factors is composite.

Worked example

To test 29, check divisibility by primes up to √29, which is about 5.39. It is not divisible by 2, 3 or 5, so 29 is prime. Testing every integer is unnecessary once a factor greater than the square root is ruled out.

Practical use

Prime testing is useful in mathematics, programming and number-theory exercises. For large numbers, efficient algorithms are preferable to manual trial division. The calculator's result depends on the range and method implemented on the page.

FAQs

Is 1 prime? No. Is 2 prime? Yes, and it is the only even prime. Why test up to the square root? Any composite factor pair must contain at least one factor no greater than the square root.

What makes a number prime?

A prime number is an integer greater than 1 with exactly two positive divisors: 1 and itself. Numbers such as 2, 3, 5, 7 and 11 are prime, while 12 is composite because it has several divisors. The number 1 is neither prime nor composite.

Worked example

To test 29, it is enough to check prime divisors up to √29, which is a little more than 5. Because 29 is not divisible by 2, 3 or 5, it is prime. This square-root limit makes trial division much faster than checking every smaller integer for large inputs.

Practical use

Prime numbers are central to number theory and are also used in computer science and cryptography. However, a simple prime checker should not be treated as a production cryptographic implementation for security-sensitive systems.
