Formula map

Density: ρ = m ÷ V. Pressure: P = F/A. Weight: W = mg. Impulse: J = FΔt. Friction: f = μN. Force: F = m × a. Average velocity: v = displacement ÷ time. Acceleration: a = change in velocity ÷ time. Kinetic energy: KE = ½mv². Potential energy: PE = mgh. Work: W = Fd cos θ. Power: P = W/t. Momentum: p = mv.

How to choose the right science calculator

Problem-first route: motion changing over time → Kinematics/Acceleration; force from mass and acceleration → Force; contact force spread over area → Pressure; mass under gravity → Weight; short-duration force interaction → Impulse; surfaces resisting relative motion → Friction; motion energy → Kinetic Energy; height-based energy → Potential Energy; force through displacement → Work; rate of energy transfer → Power; moving mass and direction → Momentum.

Use Density when mass and volume describe a material. Use Force when mass and acceleration are known or need to be related. Use Velocity for displacement over elapsed time. Use Acceleration when velocity changes over time. Use Kinetic Energy when you need the energy associated with an object's translational motion.

Units are part of the calculation

A formula can be mathematically correct and still produce a misleading result if the units are mixed. Convert values into compatible units before calculating when the tool expects a consistent unit system. Always report the result with its unit.

Worked problem-solving workflow

  1. Write down the known values and units.
  2. Identify the unknown quantity.
  3. Select the formula connecting the known and unknown values.
  4. Convert units if needed.
  5. Substitute values and calculate.
  6. Check the unit, sign and approximate magnitude.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing metres with centimetres or seconds with minutes without conversion.
  • Confusing speed with velocity when direction matters.
  • Using mass in grams with a formula expecting kilograms without conversion.
  • Forgetting that kinetic energy depends on the square of speed.
  • Using the wrong force angle in a work calculation.
  • Confusing power (W) with energy (J or Wh).
  • Ignoring velocity direction when momentum is treated as a vector.
  • Reporting a number without the unit needed to interpret it.

SCIENCE CALCULATION FRAMEWORK

Choose the model, then verify the result

A strong science calculation is more than inserting numbers into a formula. First identify the physical quantity, then choose the model that matches the problem, keep units consistent, and test whether the output is physically plausible.

1. Identify the quantity

Decide whether the unknown is a scalar such as energy, pressure or density, or a quantity whose direction matters such as velocity, acceleration, force or momentum.

2. Match the model

Use the simplest equation that actually describes the stated conditions. For example, constant-acceleration equations should not be presented as universal motion equations.

3. Check dimensions

Confirm that the units on both sides of the relationship are compatible. A unit mismatch can expose a wrong formula or an unconverted input.

4. Check the magnitude

Compare the result with the scale implied by the inputs. A mathematically valid number can still be inappropriate if the model or units were wrong.

5. Reverse-check

Where practical, substitute the calculated quantity back into the original relationship or use a related calculator to confirm the same physical relationship.

6. State the boundary

Keep idealized assumptions visible: negligible air resistance, constant acceleration, uniform material properties, ideal electrical components or other conditions should not be hidden.
