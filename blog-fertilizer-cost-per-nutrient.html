How to Calculate Fertilizer Cost per kg of Nutrient

A cheaper bag is not necessarily a cheaper source of nutrient. Compare products on a consistent nutrient basis.

Basic formula

Cost per kg nutrient = product cost ÷ kg of nutrient supplied

Example with nitrogen

If a 50 kg bag costs PKR 10,000 and contains 46% N, it supplies 23 kg N. The theoretical cost per kg N is 10,000 ÷ 23 = about PKR 434.78 per kg N.

Why bag price can mislead

A 50 kg bag of one product can contain much more or less of a particular nutrient than another 50 kg bag. Compare the nutrient you actually need, not only package price.

Cost per P₂O₅ or K₂O

Use the same method: calculate labelled nutrient kilograms from product weight and analysis, then divide product cost by nutrient kilograms.

Whole-bag versus field cost

For a field plan, calculate total product quantity and practical purchasing cost separately. Whole-bag purchasing can produce a different cash requirement from the exact field application quantity.

Other costs

Delivery, storage, spreading, labour and application losses may change the real cost of a fertilizer plan. This guide covers product-cost arithmetic only.

FERTILIZER COST • FARM DECISIONS

Practical fertilizer cost lessons beyond the bag price

Farmers compare fertilizer prices every season, but the useful comparison is usually the cost of the nutrient delivered to the field—not the sticker price of a bag by itself.

Pakistan lesson: cost pressure makes accurate nutrient accounting more important

FAO's Pakistan discussions on balanced fertilization identified fertilizer and commodity costs as important farmer concerns. A transparent nutrient-cost calculation helps compare products without assuming the cheapest bag is the cheapest nutrient source.

FAO Pakistan fertilizer stewardship

Small money-saving tricks

  • Compare cost per kg nutrient. For nitrogen, divide product cost by the kilograms of N supplied; repeat for P₂O₅ or K₂O when those are the limiting nutrients.
  • Separate exact application from whole-bag purchase. Cash paid today can exceed the exact field cost because of leftover stock.
  • Include delivery and spreading when comparing plans. Product price is only one component of field cost.
  • Keep the actual label grade in the calculation. A cost comparison based on the wrong analysis can reverse the ranking of products.
  • Record results by field. Input cost, rate and crop response become useful evidence for next season only when the records stay connected.
Best comparison: product price → nutrient kilograms → cost per nutrient → field application cost → actual field result.

Worked example: compare nutrient cost, not only bag price

Suppose Product A costs PKR 5,000 for 50 kg and contains 46% N. The bag supplies 50 × 0.46 = 23 kg N, so the nitrogen cost is about PKR 217.39 per kg N. If Product B has a different price or nitrogen analysis, repeat the same calculation before deciding which is cheaper for the nutrient you actually need.

This comparison should not be treated as a complete agronomic recommendation. A product can be cheaper per kilogram of nutrient but still be unsuitable for a particular crop, soil condition, timing window or placement method. Compare nutrient cost only after the crop and soil requirement has been established.

Farm-record trick

Keep four numbers together: product price, nutrient percentage, kilograms purchased, and kilograms actually applied. Add delivery or spreading cost when those costs differ between options. At the end of the season, compare the planned nutrient cost with the actual application record. This turns a one-time price comparison into useful farm evidence for the next season.
