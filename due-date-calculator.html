Due Date Calculator

Estimate an expected pregnancy due date from the first day of the last menstrual period using a 280-day calendar estimate.

How the due date estimate works

The standard calendar estimate adds 280 days, or 40 weeks, to the first day of the last menstrual period. It is a general dating method rather than a prediction of the exact day a baby will be born.

Worked example

If the first day of the last menstrual period is January 1, adding 280 days gives an estimated due date around October 8 in a non-leap-year example.

Why the estimate can differ

Cycle length, uncertainty about the last menstrual period, ovulation timing, and clinical dating can affect the estimated gestational age. A clinician may adjust pregnancy dating based on the available medical information and ultrasound assessment.

Important note

This calculator is for general information and is not a medical diagnosis or substitute for prenatal care. If you have questions about pregnancy dating or your health, discuss them with a qualified healthcare professional.

FAQ

Is the due date exact? No. It is an estimate; birth can occur before or after the estimated date.

What date should I enter? Enter the first day of the last menstrual period if that date is known.

Can a clinician change the estimated due date? Yes. Pregnancy dating may be reassessed using clinical information and ultrasound.

How to interpret the estimated date

The 280-day method assumes a typical 28-day menstrual cycle with ovulation around day 14. If cycles are irregular, the last menstrual period is uncertain, or ovulation occurred earlier or later, the estimate may differ. A due date is a planning estimate; it does not predict the exact day labour will begin.

What to do next

  • Use the first day of the last menstrual period only if the date is known with reasonable confidence.
  • Discuss the estimate with a qualified prenatal care professional, who can review medical history and appropriate ultrasound dating.
  • Do not use this calculator to assess symptoms, pregnancy complications or whether urgent care is needed.

If you have concerns about bleeding, severe pain, fainting or other urgent symptoms, seek prompt medical care rather than relying on a date calculator. This educational tool does not replace prenatal assessment.

How pregnancy dating estimates a due date

The traditional last-menstrual-period method counts 280 days, or 40 weeks, from the first day of the last menstrual period. It is a standardized estimate rather than a prediction of the exact birth date. The method assumes a typical cycle and ovulation timing; longer, shorter or irregular cycles can shift the estimate.

Last period versus conception date

Pregnancy weeks are conventionally counted from the last menstrual period, which is usually about two weeks before conception in a typical cycle. If a reliable conception or assisted-reproduction date is known, clinicians may use that information with the appropriate clinical dating method. Do not simply add 40 weeks to a conception date, because that uses a different starting point.

When to confirm the result

Bring the date estimate to prenatal care, especially if the last period is uncertain, cycles are irregular or the result conflicts with a clinician's assessment. Ultrasound findings and clinical history may be used to establish or revise pregnancy dating. This tool cannot assess fetal growth, pregnancy viability, symptoms or complications. Seek medical advice for individual care and urgent help for severe pain, heavy bleeding, fainting or other emergency symptoms.
