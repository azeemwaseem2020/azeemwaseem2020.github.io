Remainder formula

For integer division, the relationship is dividend = divisor × quotient + remainder. The remainder is what is left after removing complete groups of the divisor.

Worked example

For 29 ÷ 6, the quotient is 4 and the remainder is 5 because 29 = 6 × 4 + 5.

Practical uses

Remainders are useful for divisibility checks, repeating schedules and grouping items into equal-size batches. A zero remainder means the dividend divides evenly by the divisor.

Zero remainder

If the remainder is zero, the dividend is evenly divisible by the divisor. For example, 24 ÷ 6 has remainder 0. This makes the remainder operation useful for testing divisibility and identifying complete groups.

Remainder versus quotient

The quotient tells how many complete groups fit into the dividend, while the remainder tells what is left. For 29 ÷ 6, four complete groups use 24 and 5 remain. This distinction is useful in both mathematics and programming.

Divisor requirement

The divisor cannot be zero because division by zero is undefined. For integer divisibility problems, use whole-number inputs and check the remainder after division.

Quick reference

For 10 ÷ 3, the remainder is 1. For 20 ÷ 5, the remainder is 0. These simple checks illustrate the difference between an evenly divisible value and one with a remainder.

For software use, remember that negative operands can follow language-specific remainder rules, so confirm the convention used by the target language.

Remainder Calculator: Division, Modulo and Sign

The remainder is what remains after dividing one number by another. For integers, division can be written as dividend = divisor × quotient + remainder. The quotient is a whole-number count of complete groups, and the remainder records what is left over. The divisor must not be zero because division by zero is undefined.

Formula

This calculator uses JavaScript’s remainder operator (%), not a normalized non-negative modulo operation. For example, JavaScript gives −5 % 3 = −2, while a common modulo convention gives 1. For positive integers, it agrees with the familiar school remainder. For example, 17 divided by 5 gives quotient 3 and remainder 2, because 17 = 5 × 3 + 2. For negative numbers or decimal inputs, different mathematical systems may define modulo and remainder differently, so check the convention if your work depends on signs.

Worked examples

For 23 ÷ 4, the whole-number quotient is 5 and the remainder is 3, since 23 = 4 × 5 + 3. For 20 ÷ 5, the remainder is 0 because the divisor divides the dividend exactly. A zero remainder is useful for checking divisibility, repeating cycles and modular arithmetic problems.

How to use the calculator

  1. Enter the dividend, the number being divided.
  2. Enter a non-zero divisor.
  3. Calculate and inspect the remainder.
  4. For school long division, verify that the remainder's magnitude is smaller than the divisor's magnitude when using the usual integer-division convention.

Common applications

Remainders are used to determine whether a number is even or odd, assign repeating schedules, wrap an index around a circular list and solve modular arithmetic questions. For example, an integer is even when its remainder on division by 2 is zero. For clock arithmetic, 15 hours after 10 o'clock wraps around a 12-hour dial; the operation is cyclic rather than ordinary elapsed-time subtraction.

Frequently asked questions

What does a remainder of zero mean?

The dividend is divisible by the divisor with no leftover under the chosen convention.

Can the divisor be zero?

No. Division and remainder by zero are undefined.

Is remainder always positive?

Not for all programming conventions or negative operands; check the convention used by your tool or language.
