Heat transfer and temperature change

Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference. A basic heat calculation may relate transferred energy to mass, specific heat capacity and temperature change, depending on the model used.

Worked example

For 2 kg of a material with specific heat capacity 1,000 J/(kg·°C) heated by 10°C, the simple sensible-heat estimate is 20,000 J.

Check phase changes

The simple temperature-change model does not cover latent heat during melting, boiling or other phase changes. A different term is needed when a phase change occurs.

Heat FAQ

Is heat the same as temperature?

No. Temperature describes thermal state, while heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference.

Understanding each term in Q = mcΔT

Q is heat energy transferred in joules, m is mass in kilograms, c is specific heat capacity in joules per kilogram per kelvin, and ΔT is the final temperature minus the initial temperature. A temperature change of 10°C has the same size as a change of 10 K, although Celsius and kelvin have different zero points.

Choose the correct material property

Specific heat capacity depends on the material and can vary with temperature and physical state. Water's commonly used value near room temperature is about 4,186 J/(kg·K). Do not use that value for metals, oils, air or other materials; choose a suitable value for the substance and conditions in the problem.

When the simple formula is not enough

The equation estimates sensible heat when the material changes temperature without a phase change and when the specific heat can be treated as constant. Melting, freezing, boiling, heat losses to the surroundings, container heating or a changing material state may require additional terms. A negative ΔT indicates cooling under the chosen sign convention, so the calculated Q can be negative when heat leaves the material.

Heat energy formula and calculation method

For a material with approximately constant specific heat capacity and no phase change, the sensible heat relation is Q = mcΔT. Here Q is energy in joules, m is mass in kilograms, c is specific heat capacity in J/(kg·K), and ΔT = final temperature − initial temperature. A temperature difference of 1 K has the same size as a difference of 1°C, so either difference can be used when the units are consistent.

Worked example: warming water

For 2 kg of water heated from 20°C to 30°C, ΔT = 10°C and using c ≈ 4,186 J/(kg·K), Q = 2 × 4,186 × 10 = 83,720 J, or 83.72 kJ. This is the ideal heat transferred to the water; a real heater may consume more energy because heat is lost to the container and surroundings.

When Q = mcΔT is not sufficient

During melting or boiling, energy can change the phase without changing temperature; latent heat is then needed. Specific heat capacity may also vary with temperature, and heat loss, evaporation, pressure changes or chemical reactions can affect a real process. The calculator uses the entered specific heat value and does not model these effects automatically.

Heat calculator FAQs

Can heat Q be negative?

Yes. If ΔT is negative under the chosen sign convention, Q is negative, indicating that the material releases sensible heat rather than gaining it.

Is heat the same as temperature?

No. Temperature describes thermal state; heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference.

Can I use Celsius in the formula?

Use a temperature difference, not an absolute Celsius reading. A change of 10°C is numerically the same as a change of 10 K.
