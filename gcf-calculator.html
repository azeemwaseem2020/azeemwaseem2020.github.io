Understanding the greatest common factor

The greatest common factor (GCF), also called the greatest common divisor, is the largest positive integer that divides two or more whole numbers without leaving a remainder. For example, the factors of 18 include 1, 2, 3, 6, 9 and 18, while the factors of 24 include 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12 and 24. Their largest shared factor is 6.

Worked example

For 48 and 60, prime factorization gives 48 = 2⁴ × 3 and 60 = 2² × 3 × 5. Take the smallest exponent of each common prime: 2² × 3 = 12. The calculator can therefore reduce a fraction such as 48/60 to 4/5 by dividing both numbers by 12.

A practical Pakistan example

Suppose a school in Pakistan has 84 exercise books and 126 notebooks to divide into identical packs with no items left over. GCF(84, 126) = 42, so 42 identical packs are possible if each pack receives the same number of each item. This is a planning example; the actual packing rules still determine whether that arrangement is useful.

Why GCF matters

GCF is useful for simplifying fractions, grouping objects evenly and solving problems where the largest equal grouping is required. Euclid's algorithm is an efficient method for larger numbers: repeatedly divide and use the remainder until the remainder becomes zero; the last non-zero remainder is the GCF.

Common mistakes

GCF is not the same as least common multiple (LCM). GCF looks for the largest shared divisor, while LCM looks for the smallest positive number that is a multiple of all inputs.

GCF FAQ

Can the GCF be 1? Yes. Numbers with no common factor greater than 1 are relatively prime.

Can GCF be found for more than two numbers? Yes. Find the GCF of two numbers, then use that result with the next number.

Is GCF the same as GCD? Yes. Greatest common factor and greatest common divisor describe the same concept.

Does GCF work with negative integers? The conventional GCF is reported as a positive integer using the absolute values of the inputs.
