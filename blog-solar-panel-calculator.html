SOLAR PANEL SIZING GUIDE

Solar Panel Calculator: How Many Panels Do I Need?

A useful panel estimate starts with electricity consumption and system output assumptions, not simply the size of the home.

Quick method: estimate daily kWh, divide by peak sun hours and an efficiency assumption to get a first-pass PV capacity, then divide the required watts by your chosen panel wattage and round up.

Solar panel formula

PV size (kW) ≈ daily energy (kWh) ÷ (peak sun hours × system efficiency).

Then calculate panel count = required PV watts ÷ panel watts. For example, a 5,000 W target using 550 W panels gives 9.09 panels, so the arithmetic result must be rounded up to 10 panels. This does not mean every 10-panel system will produce exactly the target energy.

Worked example

Suppose a home needs about 20 kWh per day. If a planning assumption is 5 peak-sun-hour equivalent and 80% system efficiency, the first-pass PV size is 20 ÷ (5 × 0.8) = 5 kW. With 550 W panels, 5,000 ÷ 550 = 9.09, so 10 panels would be needed by simple panel-count rounding.

Start with real electricity use

Recent electricity bills are a useful starting point for estimating monthly kWh. Divide a representative month's usage by the number of days in that period for a daily estimate. The bill amount in currency is less useful because tariffs can include fixed charges, taxes and different rate slabs.

Peak load is a separate check

Panel capacity and inverter capacity answer different questions. Panel sizing is mainly about energy production, while inverter sizing also has to consider the power drawn by appliances at the same time. High-load equipment such as air conditioners, pumps and electric heaters can make peak demand much higher than average demand.

Roof space and panel layout

Panel count is only part of the physical design. Usable roof area must allow for access paths, shade, mounting clearances, water tanks, parapets and other obstructions. A higher-wattage panel can reduce the number of panels, but electrical compatibility, dimensions, availability and cost still matter.

Why calculator results differ

Two solar calculators can give different answers because they use different assumptions for daily energy, sun hours, system losses, panel wattage or desired production. Compare the assumptions before deciding that one result is wrong.

Pakistan-focused planning

For a home in Pakistan, consider seasonal cooling demand, daytime versus evening usage, roof dust, shading and the intended grid or battery arrangement. If backup storage is planned, define which appliances must run during an outage instead of automatically sizing the battery for every connected load.

Before buying panels

  1. Review several months of electricity consumption.
  2. List high-power appliances and realistic operating hours.
  3. Estimate daily energy separately from peak load.
  4. Check usable, reasonably unshaded roof area.
  5. Confirm panel wattage, inverter limits and electrical compatibility.
  6. Ask for a site-specific production and installation assessment.

Important limitation

This method is a planning estimate. Actual PV output depends on location, weather, temperature, shading, orientation, equipment characteristics, wiring and installation quality. A final system should be checked against the actual site and equipment specifications.

Frequently asked questions

What does this calculator help estimate?

It applies the stated inputs and standard formula to produce an estimate. The result should be checked against the equipment specification, local conditions and the assumptions used.

Are the results exact?

No calculation is exact when real-world inputs vary. Treat the result as a planning estimate and verify important decisions with current technical specifications.

Which inputs should I check first?

Check the units, rated values, system conditions and any losses or efficiency assumptions before relying on the final result.
