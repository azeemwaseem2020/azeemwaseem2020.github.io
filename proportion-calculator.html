Solving proportions

A proportion states that two ratios are equal. It is useful when a quantity changes at a constant rate and the relationship between the known values should be preserved. The important step is identifying which quantities belong in corresponding positions; simply multiplying numbers without preserving that relationship can create a plausible but incorrect result.

Formula

For a/b = c/d, cross multiplication gives ad = bc. If x is unknown in a/b = c/x, then x = bc/a, provided a is not zero. Units should be compatible before comparing ratios.

Worked example

If 4 kg of a material costs Rs 1,200, the proportional cost for 7 kg is 1,200 ÷ 4 × 7 = Rs 2,100 under a constant unit-price assumption. The unit price is Rs 300/kg.

Pakistan-specific example

A farmer estimating fertilizer quantity for a larger field can use a proportion only when the recommended rate scales linearly with area. If a recommendation is 50 kg for one acre, a simple proportional calculation gives 150 kg for three acres, but the agronomic recommendation itself should come from the relevant crop and soil guidance.

Common questions

Does a proportion always scale linearly? No. It is appropriate only when the relationship is assumed proportional.

Can units differ? Convert them before forming the ratio.

Why use cross multiplication? It removes the denominators and creates an equivalent equation.

Can zero be a denominator? No. Division by zero is undefined.

Checking proportional relationships

A proportion states that two ratios are equal. For a:b = c:d, cross multiplication gives a×d = b×c. If three values are known, the fourth can be solved directly. Keep units consistent before comparing ratios.

For example, if 3 notebooks cost PKR 450, the proportional cost of 5 notebooks is 450×5/3 = PKR 750, assuming the unit price remains constant. Real purchases may include bulk discounts or fixed charges, so proportionality is an assumption that should be checked.
