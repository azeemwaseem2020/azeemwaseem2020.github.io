Ohm's Law: Voltage, Current and Resistance

Ohm's law relates voltage V, current I and resistance R through V = IR. Rearranging gives I = V/R and R = V/I. Use volts, amperes and ohms for the standard SI form. For example, 12 V across a 6 Ω resistor produces 2 A because 12 ÷ 6 = 2.

The electrical power associated with a resistive load can also be written P = VI = I²R = V²/R. A 12 V, 6 Ω load draws 2 A and consumes 24 W under the ideal resistive assumption. This connection is useful when checking whether a power supply or resistor rating is suitable.

In a Pakistan household or workshop example, a low-voltage DC device drawing 3 A at 12 V uses 36 W. The wire, adapter and protection device must still be selected according to actual current, cable length, voltage drop and safety requirements. Ohm's law alone does not determine safe wiring.

Where it does not directly apply

Ohm's law describes the relationship for an ohmic component under the stated conditions. LEDs, batteries, motors and other nonlinear devices can have voltage-current behaviour that is not a simple constant resistance. Temperature can also change resistance.

FAQs

What happens if resistance doubles? At fixed voltage, current halves.

Can I calculate power too? Yes, when voltage and current or resistance are known.

Is every electrical device ohmic? No. Many components have nonlinear characteristics.
