How Much Does It Cost to Run an Air Conditioner?

The cost of running an AC depends on its electrical input, how long it actively cools, and the price you pay per kilowatt-hour (kWh). A unit’s BTU rating describes cooling capacity, not the electricity it draws from the wall.

Written and maintained by Abdul Qadir · Calculation methodology

Use the Air Conditioner Electricity Cost Calculator to estimate cost per hour, day, 30-day month and cooling season. It accepts input watts directly or estimates input watts from cooling capacity and EER.

1. Find electrical input watts, not just BTU

Air conditioners are often described by cooling capacity, such as 8,000, 10,000 or 12,000 BTU per hour. That number tells you how much heat the unit can remove under stated test conditions; it is not the same as electrical power consumption. Look for “rated input,” “power input,” or watts on the nameplate or technical specification sheet. If the manufacturer provides energy use in kWh for a defined period, that can be more useful than a rough wattage estimate.

If you know cooling capacity in BTU/h and an EER rating, a rough rated-input estimate is: electrical watts ≈ cooling capacity (BTU/h) ÷ EER (BTU per watt-hour). For example, 12,000 BTU/h divided by EER 10 gives approximately 1,200 W. This is a rating-based approximation, not a guarantee of actual draw. Do not enter a SEER value into an EER field; seasonal and instantaneous efficiency ratings are not interchangeable.

2. Calculate kWh used while cooling

Convert watts to kilowatts by dividing by 1,000. Then multiply by the number of hours the unit is actively using that power. A 1,200 W unit running at full input for 8 hours uses 1.2 × 8 = 9.6 kWh. If it cycles on and off or an inverter compressor varies its speed, the average energy can be lower than the full-load calculation. A duty-cycle percentage can model that behavior as an estimate, but measured energy is better when available.

3. Multiply energy by your electricity rate

Estimated energy cost = kWh used × price per kWh. If the unit uses 6.72 kWh in a day and your tariff is $0.18 per kWh, the energy cost is 6.72 × $0.18 = $1.21 per day. At the same schedule, that is about $36.29 over 30 days. These are illustrative figures; change the rate to match your own bill. The currency label does not convert money, so use a tariff and currency from the same country.

4. Estimate a cooling season, not just one day

For a seasonal estimate, multiply the daily energy cost by the number of days the AC runs. A useful planning approach is to enter days per week and weeks per year, then compare a lower-runtime, expected-runtime and high-runtime scenario. For example, 6 hours a day for 12 hot weeks can produce a very different seasonal total from 10 hours a day for 24 weeks. Weather, occupancy, room insulation, thermostat setting and the number of very hot days all affect actual usage.

5. Understand what makes AC costs vary

  • Electrical input and efficiency: use the exact model’s input rating when possible.
  • Runtime and compressor cycling: hours switched on do not always equal hours at full power.
  • Outdoor conditions and heat gain: hot weather, sunlight, air leaks and poor insulation can increase cooling demand.
  • Maintenance and installation: blocked filters or poor installation may affect performance; follow the manufacturer’s maintenance instructions.
  • Tariff structure: time-of-use pricing, tiered rates, taxes and delivery charges may change the final bill.

6. Does the calculator show the full electricity bill?

No. An appliance running-cost estimate prices the energy used by the air conditioner. It does not automatically include a household’s fixed service charge, standing charge, taxes, delivery fees or the cost of other appliances. Do not add a fee twice if it is already included in an all-in rate. For a household bill estimate, use the US Electricity Bill Calculator; for other devices, use the Electricity Cost Calculator. If your utility charges different rates for usage blocks, the Tiered Electricity Rate Calculator can model a simple tiered plan.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to run an AC for one hour?

Multiply the unit’s electrical input in kW by its active runtime in hours and by your price per kWh. A 1.2 kW unit at $0.18/kWh costs about $0.22 for one full-load hour; actual cost varies with power draw and cycling.

How many kWh does a 12,000 BTU air conditioner use?

BTU/h alone is not enough to know. With an EER of 10, rated input would be approximately 1,200 W, or 1.2 kWh per full-power hour. Use the exact unit label and operating conditions for a better estimate.

Can I use the calculator in the US, UK or Europe?

Yes. Enter your own rate per kWh and choose the matching currency label. The tool does not fetch live tariffs or exchange rates.

Will an inverter AC always use less electricity?

Not in every situation. Consumption depends on the model, load, sizing, settings and outdoor conditions. Compare verified energy data for specific models and use measured kWh when available.

Note: This is an educational estimate, not a guarantee of consumption or a recommendation to change electrical equipment. Follow the manufacturer’s safety instructions.
