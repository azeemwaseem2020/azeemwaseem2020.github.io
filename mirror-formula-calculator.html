Mirror Formula Calculator: Focal Length and Image Distance

The mirror equation relates focal length, object distance and image distance for an ideal spherical mirror under the paraxial-ray approximation. Because textbooks may use different sign conventions, always interpret positive and negative distances according to the convention your course uses. The equation is useful for checking whether three supplied values are consistent or solving for one missing value.

Formula

1/f = 1/do + 1/di, where f is focal length, do is object distance and di is image distance. Rearranging gives f = (do × di) ÷ (do + di), do = (f × di) ÷ (di − f), and di = (f × do) ÷ (do − f), when the denominators are non-zero. A zero denominator represents a special limiting case rather than an ordinary finite result.

Worked example

Using the magnitude form for a concave mirror, if f = 0.20 m and do = 0.40 m, then 1/di = 1/f − 1/do = 5 − 2.5 = 2.5 per metre, so di = 0.40 m. This is the familiar case where an object is placed at twice the focal length and the real image forms at twice the focal length. Sign interpretation still depends on the chosen convention.

How to use the calculator

  1. Enter exactly two valid non-zero quantities if you want to solve for the third.
  2. Use one consistent distance unit for all entries.
  3. Leave the unknown field blank rather than entering a guessed value.
  4. If checking three values, compare the reciprocal sums and inspect the sign convention.

Interpretation limits

The simple mirror equation assumes an idealized spherical mirror and rays close to the principal axis. Large-angle rays, mirror aberrations and real-world measurement errors can cause experimental results to differ. Do not mix the magnitude convention in one textbook with the Cartesian sign convention from another. A calculator can verify algebra, but a ray diagram is often the best way to understand whether an image is real or virtual and upright or inverted.

Frequently asked questions

Why does the calculator ask for two values?

The mirror equation has three related quantities; two determine the third when the case is mathematically valid.

Can focal length be zero?

Not for an ordinary finite spherical mirror model; zero causes the reciprocal equation to be undefined.

Does a negative image distance always mean an error?

No. Its meaning depends on the sign convention and whether the image is virtual.

Mirror Formula Calculator: Sign Convention and Image Type

The spherical mirror equation relates focal length f, object distance u and image distance v. A common form is 1/f = 1/u + 1/v, but signs depend on the convention used by the textbook or calculator. Do not mix distances entered under one convention with formulas from another; the same physical mirror can produce positive or negative signed distances under different conventions.

Rearranging the formula

Solving for image distance gives 1/v = 1/f − 1/u, so v = fu ÷ (u − f), provided the denominator is not zero. When the object distance equals the focal length in this simplified model, the image distance tends toward infinity and the formula has no finite result.

Worked example

Using the unsigned arithmetic form with f = 20 cm and u = 40 cm gives 1/v = 1/20 − 1/40 = 1/40, so v = 40 cm. Whether that result is labelled real or virtual depends on mirror type and sign convention; confirm those assumptions before interpreting the sign.

How to use it

  1. Check the calculator's stated sign convention.
  2. Enter focal length and object distance in the same units.
  3. Do not enter zero for a distance used in a denominator.
  4. Interpret the result together with mirror type and image orientation.

The thin spherical mirror model is an approximation for paraxial rays and idealized geometry. Real mirrors may introduce aberrations, and object placement affects image orientation and size. Use a ray diagram as a cross-check when learning the concept.

FAQs

Why can my textbook show a different sign?

Different sign conventions assign positive and negative directions differently; apply one convention consistently.

What happens when the object is at the focal point?

The idealized image distance tends toward infinity, so there is no finite image distance from the rearranged equation.

Can I mix centimetres and metres?

Convert all distances to the same unit before calculating.
