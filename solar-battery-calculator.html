Formula, example and guide

Formula

Battery capacity is estimated from the required energy, system voltage and usable depth of discharge. Allow for inverter and other system losses rather than treating the calculated number as an exact battery specification.

Size from the real backup job

Start with the equipment that must stay powered and the required backup duration. The calculator then accounts for usable discharge and conversion efficiency.

Do not choose a battery from Ah alone. Power, surge, chemistry, temperature, aging and inverter compatibility also matter.

1. Why this number is calculated

Delivered energy = load (kW) × backup hours. Nominal battery energy is estimated as delivered energy ÷ [usable depth of discharge × efficiency]. Approximate Ah equals nominal battery watt-hours ÷ battery voltage.

Example: 1,000 W for 4 hours needs 4.00 kWh delivered. At 80% usable discharge and 90% efficiency, the planning estimate is about 5.56 kWh nominal storage, or about 116 Ah at 48 V.

2. Assumptions

  • Backup load: assumes the stated load represents the average power that must be supported.
  • Backup hours: defines how long the selected loads must run.
  • Depth of discharge: represents the portion of nominal storage you plan to use.
  • Efficiency: is a simplified planning allowance for conversion losses.
  • Voltage: converts energy to approximate Ah; it does not prove electrical compatibility.

3. Lithium vs lead-acid

Battery chemistry affects usable capacity, discharge behavior, temperature sensitivity, maintenance, charging limits and aging. Two batteries with the same nominal Ah can therefore behave differently in a real installation.

Use the manufacturer's usable-energy and discharge specifications for final selection. Do not assume one universal depth-of-discharge value works for every battery.

4. Motors and starting surge

Refrigerators, pumps, fans, compressors and other motors can have higher starting demand than their running wattage. This calculator estimates energy; the inverter and battery must also support the required continuous and peak power.

5. Temperature and aging

Battery performance can change with temperature, age, charge state and discharge rate. A headline capacity should not be treated as guaranteed usable energy under every operating condition.

6. Do you need a battery in Pakistan?

Not always. A battery is most useful when you need backup during outages, want stored solar energy after sunset, or have a system strategy that benefits from higher self-consumption. A grid-connected daytime-use system can have a different battery requirement from an off-grid or backup-focused system.

7. Battery and inverter checks

  • Confirm battery voltage compatibility.
  • Check continuous and peak discharge current.
  • Check supported battery chemistry and manufacturer requirements.
  • Confirm charging limits and expected recharge time.
  • Check inverter backup-load and surge limits.
  • Allow for temperature, aging and future load changes.

Common mistakes

  • Sizing for the whole house when only selected loads need backup.
  • Using Ah without checking voltage and usable energy.
  • Ignoring motor starting demand.
  • Assuming all advertised capacity is always usable.
  • Ignoring temperature and aging.
  • Choosing a battery before checking inverter compatibility.

FAQ

Can I use this for lithium batteries?

Yes, for first-pass planning. Follow the manufacturer's usable-energy, current, temperature and BMS requirements for final selection.

What about lead-acid?

Use manufacturer discharge limits and performance data because practical capacity depends on the application and discharge rate.

Does a larger battery always mean better backup?

No. The battery must be matched with the inverter, charging source, loads and required backup strategy.
