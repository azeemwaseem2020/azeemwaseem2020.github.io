CALCORA METHOD

How we build and check calculators

A calculator is only useful when the formula, units and assumptions are understandable. This page explains the checks Calcora uses so visitors can inspect a result instead of simply trusting a number.

1. Define the exact question

Each tool starts with a specific calculation job. Inputs are kept focused on values that materially affect the result. If a real-world decision needs information the calculator cannot know, that boundary is stated on the page.

2. State the calculation method

Where practical, the calculator shows the governing formula, conversion or calculation steps. Units are made explicit so users can compare the input and output on the same basis.

3. Test known examples

Important calculators are checked against hand calculations, algebraic rearrangement, known examples or independently calculated test cases before release. Edge cases such as zero, negative, empty or impossible inputs are also considered where relevant.

4. Separate arithmetic from changing facts

Calcora performs calculations from the values a visitor supplies. Current interest rates, taxes, PSX rules, market prices, electricity tariffs, equipment specifications, weather, soil conditions and agricultural recommendations can change and should be verified from current authoritative sources.

5. Explain limitations

A result can be mathematically correct while still being unsuitable for a particular real-world decision. Calculator pages therefore aim to identify important assumptions, unit limits and situations where professional or current-source verification is appropriate.

6. Make corrections visible

If a user finds a broken calculation, unclear assumption or incorrect result, Calcora encourages a report through the contact page. A useful report includes the calculator name, inputs, expected result and actual result.

Source hierarchy for changing information

  1. Official government, regulator or exchange documents for rules, taxes and regulatory information.
  2. Manufacturer or product documentation for equipment specifications.
  3. University, extension or recognized technical sources for agricultural and scientific guidance.
  4. Primary financial or market sources for current market information.
  5. Secondary sources are used cautiously and are not treated as authority when a primary source is available.

How a new calculator is reviewed

Before a new tool is treated as part of the calculator library, its input definitions, units, calculation path and representative examples are reviewed. Where a result depends on a changing external value, that dependency is identified instead of being presented as a permanent fact.

What this means for users

Use the calculator to understand the arithmetic and compare scenarios. For consequential decisions, verify the inputs and current rules before acting. Calcora does not present an estimate as a guarantee.

CALCORA QUALITY STANDARD

How to read a calculator result

Calcora calculators are built around explicit inputs, stated formulas and visible assumptions. For important results, check units, review the formula and compare the output with a simple back-of-the-envelope calculation.

What we check

  • Inputs: labels, units and required values are clear.
  • Formula: the mathematical relationship matches the stated calculation.
  • Rounding: displayed precision is not mistaken for measurement accuracy.
  • Edge cases: invalid or impossible inputs are handled deliberately.
  • Context: tax, finance, agriculture and health results can depend on rules beyond the arithmetic.

Search by the real question

“What is 20% of 500?”, “500 increased by 20%”, and “500 is 20% of what?” require different operations. The same principle applies to EMI, PSX, solar, fertilizer and TMR calculations.
