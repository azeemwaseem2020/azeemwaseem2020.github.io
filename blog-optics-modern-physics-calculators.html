Optics & Modern Physics Calculators: A Problem-First Guide

Map common classroom questions to focused calculators while keeping sign conventions and assumptions visible.

Optics

Use the Lens Formula Calculator for focal length and image-distance relationships, Mirror Formula Calculator for spherical-mirror problems, Magnification Calculator for image-size relationships, and Snell's Law Calculator for refraction.

Modern physics

Photon Energy connects frequency or wavelength to photon energy. Mass–Energy calculates rest-energy equivalence. Half-Life models repeated fractional decay.

How to choose the right optics calculator

Start with the physical relationship in the question rather than the number you want to find. A thin-lens problem belongs with the Lens Formula Calculator; a spherical-mirror problem with the Mirror Formula Calculator; a question about image size or orientation with the Magnification Calculator; and a change in direction between two media with Snell's Law Calculator. This keeps the formula matched to the physical model.

Optics: sign conventions matter

Lens and mirror calculations can give an apparently incorrect answer when the arithmetic is fine but the sign convention is inconsistent. Write down the convention used by the problem or calculator before entering values. Keep object distance, image distance and focal length consistent, and interpret a negative or positive result according to that convention rather than changing its sign simply because the answer looks unexpected.

Worked example: lens formula

For a thin lens, one common Cartesian form is 1/f = 1/v − 1/u. If f = 10 cm and u = −30 cm, then 1/v = 1/10 + 1/(−30) = 1/15, giving v = 15 cm. The numerical result is meaningful only when the same sign convention is used throughout.

Refraction: keep the media in the correct order

Snell's law is n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂, where the angles are measured from the normal. For a ray moving from air into glass, use the refractive index of air for n₁ and glass for n₂. Entering the media in reverse order can produce a mathematically valid but physically wrong result.

Photon energy: wavelength and frequency

Photon energy follows E = hf and E = hc/λ. If wavelength is entered in nanometres, convert it to metres when using SI values for h and c. For example, a 500 nm photon has an energy of about 3.98 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. The same calculation can be expressed in electronvolts with the appropriate conversion.

Mass-energy and half-life

Mass-energy equivalence uses E = mc², so even a small mass corresponds to a large energy because c is squared. Half-life follows a different model: N = N₀(1/2)^(t/T₁/₂). After one half-life 50% remains, after two 25%, and after three 12.5%. This is exponential decay, not a fixed quantity removed at every interval.

Common mistakes to check

  • Confirm that every distance, wavelength and angle uses the expected unit.
  • Check whether an angle is measured from the normal or from the surface.
  • Do not mix sign conventions between a textbook example and the calculator.
  • For half-life, distinguish elapsed time from the half-life value itself.
  • Keep constants and units consistent before rounding the final result.

Frequently asked questions

Which calculator should I use for a lens problem?

Use the Lens Formula Calculator for focal length, object distance or image distance. Use the Magnification Calculator when the main question concerns image size or orientation.

Why can a mirror or lens answer have a negative distance?

A negative value can be meaningful under the chosen sign convention. It may indicate the location or nature of the image rather than an arithmetic error.

What units should I use for photon energy?

Use a consistent unit system. With h in joule-seconds and c in metres per second, wavelength should be in metres and the result is in joules.

Is half-life the same as subtracting half each time?

No. The amount remaining is halved at each half-life, producing exponential decay. The percentage removed therefore changes as the remaining amount becomes smaller.
