Sector area: formula and practical guide

A circular sector is the part of a circle enclosed by two radii and the arc between them. It resembles a slice of pizza. Sector area depends on the circle’s radius and the central angle, which must be entered in degrees in this calculator.

Formula

Sector area = (angle ÷ 360) × π × radius². The full circle is 360°, so the angle divided by 360 gives the fraction of the circle represented by the sector. Multiplying that fraction by πr² gives the sector’s area.

Worked example

For a radius of 6 cm and a central angle of 90°, area = (90 ÷ 360) × π × 6² = 9π ≈ 28.2743 cm². A 90° sector is one quarter of the full circle, which is a useful way to check the answer.

How to use the calculator

  1. Measure the radius from the circle’s centre to its edge; do not enter the diameter.
  2. Enter the central angle in degrees, not radians.
  3. Select Calculate and read the area.
  4. Label the answer with square units based on the radius unit—for example, cm² if radius is in centimetres.

Common mistakes and limitations

If you know the diameter, divide it by two to get the radius. The radius is squared, so doubling it makes the full-circle and sector areas four times as large when the angle stays fixed. This formula assumes a flat circle and a central angle measured at its centre. It does not calculate arc length or perimeter; those require separate formulas. Use a consistent length unit and round only after calculating.

Frequently asked questions

Can the angle be more than 180°?

Yes. A sector can represent a larger portion of a circle; 270° is three quarters of a full circle. For a standard single revolution, use an angle from 0° through 360°.

Is sector area the same as arc length?

No. Sector area measures the two-dimensional slice, while arc length measures the curved boundary segment. Area uses square units and arc length uses linear units.

What if I have the diameter?

Divide the diameter by two before entering it as the radius. Entering diameter in the radius field makes the calculated area four times too large.
