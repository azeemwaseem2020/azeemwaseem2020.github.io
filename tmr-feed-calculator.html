How the calculator works

  1. Choose the batch size you want to prepare.
  2. Enter the number of animals and the planned as-fed feed amount per animal per day.
  3. Enter each ingredient and its percentage. The total should be 100%.
  4. Optionally add dry-matter percentage and current price/kg.
  5. Press Calculate my TMR to get the exact batch quantities.

Ingredient weight = batch weight × ingredient percentage ÷ 100. For example, 25% of a 1,000 kg batch is 250 kg. The calculator also checks that the entered mix totals 100% before treating it as a complete batch.

Dry matter versus as-fed weight

Dairy TMRs are commonly formulated on a dry-matter basis while ingredients are physically loaded on an as-fed basis. Moisture can change the physical weight needed to deliver the same dry matter. Penn State and University of Minnesota guidance both emphasize checking forage/feed dry matter because changes can alter the amount loaded into a mixer.

For a simple conversion, as-fed kg = dry-matter kg ÷ DM fraction. If a target is 100 kg DM and an ingredient is 40% DM, the arithmetic as-fed quantity is 250 kg. This is a conversion example, not a ration recommendation.

Easy batch scaling

For a 25% ingredient, 500 kg gives 125 kg, 1,000 kg gives 250 kg and 2,000 kg gives 500 kg. The calculator scales the same formulation to the selected batch size.

Daily feed use and batch coverage

Group feed use = number of animals × as-fed kg per animal per day. Batch coverage = batch weight ÷ daily group feed. These are mathematical planning outputs; the feeding rate itself must come from an appropriate ration.

Monitor actual dry matter intake

For a stronger farm check, compare what was delivered with what was refused, account for TMR dry matter, and divide the dry matter consumed by the number of animals. Penn State Extension describes this approach for monitoring actual dry matter intake and notes that changes in forage dry matter can materially change delivered nutrient supply.

Competitor calculators and ration tools commonly ask for species, production stage, body weight, milk yield, feed availability or feeding goal before producing an estimate. What professional TMR practice checks

  • Current forage and feed analysis, especially dry matter for silage and other wet feeds.
  • Accurate mixer and scale weights and consistent mixing.
  • Animal group, production level and stage of lactation.
  • Energy, crude protein, fibre, minerals, vitamins and other relevant nutrient targets.
  • Actual intake and refusals compared with the formulated plan.

University extension guidance notes that TMR consistency and feed dry matter are important because changes in ingredient moisture or loading can change the delivered ration.

TMR for dairy cows and buffalo

Worked example: scale a 1,000 kg TMR batch

Suppose your existing formulation is 40% maize silage, 20% green fodder, 30% concentrate mix and 10% wheat straw. For a 1,000 kg batch, the calculator converts those percentages to 400 kg, 200 kg, 300 kg and 100 kg respectively. If 20 animals are fed 20 kg as-fed per day, the group needs 400 kg/day, so a 1,000 kg batch covers 2.5 days. This example demonstrates the arithmetic only; it is not a recommended ration.

Before you trust the result

1. Confirm the formulation

Ingredient percentages should represent the formulation you actually intend to mix and should total 100%.

2. Check dry matter

Use current dry-matter values for wet feeds where possible. A change in moisture can change the as-fed kilograms needed.

3. Check the mixer

Compare entered quantities with the actual scale readings and keep the weighing process consistent.

4. Check the feed bunk

Compare delivered feed and refusals and watch for sorting rather than assuming the formulated ration is the ration consumed.

Practical TMR tips for farmers

  • Use the same basis: do not mix dry-matter percentages with as-fed percentages in one formulation.
  • Check feed dry matter: moisture changes can change the as-fed amount needed to deliver the same dry matter.
  • Watch refusals: compare feed delivered with feed left over to understand actual intake.
  • Keep groups consistent: TMR works best when animals in a group have reasonably similar feeding requirements.

These are management principles, not a substitute for a professionally balanced ration. University extension guidance emphasizes monitoring dry matter, animal numbers and daily intake when managing TMR.

How to use a TMR calculation safely on the farm

A TMR calculator is most useful for checking quantities and scaling a ration that has already been defined. The arithmetic can tell you how much of each ingredient is needed for a batch, but it does not independently decide whether that ration is nutritionally appropriate for a particular cow, buffalo or production group.

  1. Record the intended ingredient proportions and whether values are as-fed or dry matter basis.
  2. Enter the target batch size and number of animals.
  3. Check the resulting ingredient weights against the intended mix.
  4. Compare actual refusals and dry matter intake with the farm's records.
  5. Review nutrient requirements with a qualified nutritionist or veterinarian when changing a ration materially.

Ingredient dry matter, forage quality, animal stage, milk production, body condition, water availability and management conditions can all affect a practical feeding decision.

FAQ

Does this make a complete dairy ration?

No. It calculates quantities from a known formulation and performs arithmetic checks. Nutritional balancing requires appropriate animal and feed data.

Can I use it for buffalo?

Yes for quantity planning from a known buffalo formulation. It does not create a buffalo-specific nutrient prescription.

Can I calculate TMR cost?

Yes. Enter a price per kg for ingredients when you have current local prices. The tool estimates batch cost from the entered ingredient weights and prices.

Why is dry matter important?

Because two feeds with the same as-fed weight can contain different amounts of dry matter. Changing moisture can change the physical amount required to deliver a dry-matter target.
