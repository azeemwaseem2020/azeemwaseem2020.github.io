Worked volume examples

A rectangular box that is 10 cm long, 5 cm wide and 4 cm high has a volume of 10 × 5 × 4 = 200 cm³. A cube with a side length of 3 m has a volume of 3³ = 27 m³. A cylinder with radius 2 cm and height 6 cm has a volume of π × 2² × 6 = 24π, approximately 75.40 cm³. The calculator returns cubic units because each dimension contributes a length unit.

Volume and capacity are related but different labels

Volume describes three-dimensional space and is expressed in cubic units such as cm³ or m³. Capacity often describes how much liquid or material a container can hold and may be expressed in litres. One litre is equal to 1,000 cubic centimetres, and one cubic metre is equal to 1,000 litres. Convert only after calculating with compatible dimensions, and remember that a container's usable capacity can be lower than its geometric volume because of wall thickness, headspace or its shape.

Choose dimensions carefully

For a cylinder, enter the radius rather than the diameter. If you know the diameter, divide it by two first. For a rectangular box, length, width and height must refer to perpendicular dimensions of the same shape. This calculator covers rectangular boxes, cubes and cylinders; it does not directly calculate irregular objects, cones or spheres. For irregular shapes, use an appropriate geometric model or a measurement method suited to the object.

Common volume unit conversions

Because volume is three-dimensional, conversion factors are cubed. One metre is 100 centimetres, but one cubic metre is 1,000,000 cubic centimetres. For practical capacity comparisons, 1,000 cm³ equals 1 litre and 1 m³ equals 1,000 litres. Do not convert cubic units using the linear factor alone. The calculator reports a numeric result in cubic units based on the dimensions you enter; it does not automatically label the answer as litres or gallons. Convert the result separately when needed and state the unit clearly. For containers, tanks or packaging, use measured internal dimensions if the goal is usable capacity, and allow for rounded dimensions and real-world tolerances.

Practical volume calculation: dimensions, examples and unit checks

Volume measures three-dimensional space. Choose a shape that reasonably matches the object, then enter dimensions in the same unit. For a rectangular box, use V = length × width × height. For a cube, use V = side³. For a cylinder, use V = π × radius² × height. The result is in cubic units: dimensions in metres produce cubic metres, while dimensions in centimetres produce cubic centimetres.

Worked example: a rectangular tank

A tank with internal dimensions of 2 m long, 1.5 m wide and 0.5 m high has a geometric volume of 2 × 1.5 × 0.5 = 1.5 m³. Since 1 m³ equals 1,000 litres, its theoretical capacity is 1,500 litres. Actual usable capacity may be lower because of freeboard, internal fittings, rounded corners or a maximum fill line. For a tank, measure the internal dimensions if the aim is to estimate liquid capacity.

Example: a cylindrical container

If a cylinder has a radius of 0.4 m and a height of 1 m, its volume is π × 0.4² × 1, approximately 0.503 m³, or about 503 litres. Check whether the measurement you have is a radius or a diameter: the radius is half the diameter. Entering the diameter as the radius makes the calculated circular area—and therefore the volume—four times too large.

When the result is an estimate

Irregular objects may not fit a perfect box, cylinder or cube. You may need to divide the object into simpler shapes, measure liquid displacement, or use a suitable engineering method. For construction materials, geometric volume is not the same as purchase quantity: compaction, voids, spillage and cutting losses can change the amount to order. For a room or storage container, note whether dimensions are internal or external.

Final checks: keep all dimensions in a consistent unit, verify radius versus diameter, and convert cubic units using cubic—not linear—conversion factors. Record the shape, dimensions and unit beside the answer so another person can reproduce the calculation.

Volume versus usable capacity

A geometric volume describes the space inside an ideal shape. A container’s usable capacity may be lower because it should not be filled to the rim, or because pipes, fittings and internal structures take up space. For a water tank, calculate from internal dimensions and observe the marked safe fill level.

Convert cubic units correctly

Unit conversions for volume are cubed: 1 m³ = 1,000 litres and 1 cm³ = 1 mL. Do not use a length conversion factor only once. For example, 1 m = 100 cm, but 1 m³ = 100 × 100 × 100 = 1,000,000 cm³.

Planning materials

For concrete, soil or aggregate, geometric volume is a starting point rather than a guaranteed order quantity. Ground conditions, compaction, voids and handling losses can change the required amount. Confirm material-specific guidance before purchasing.
