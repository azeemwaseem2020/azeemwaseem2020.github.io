How refinance break-even works

Refinancing costs matter because a lower payment does not automatically mean a lower overall cost. A simple break-even estimate divides the refinancing cost by the monthly payment savings; changing the new term can also change total interest.

Worked refinance example

Suppose the current monthly principal-and-interest payment is 1,900 and a new loan would reduce it to 1,750. The apparent monthly saving is 150. If closing costs are 3,000, simple break-even is 3,000 ÷ 150 = 20 months. This is a cash-flow estimate, not proof that refinancing lowers total borrowing cost.

What this estimate leaves out

  • Changes to the remaining loan term and the date the loan is paid off.
  • Prepayment penalties, points, appraisal, legal and lender fees unless included in the entered cost.
  • Escrow, property taxes, insurance and other costs not included in principal and interest.
  • Variable rates, fees added to the balance, and differences in future payment timing.

Compare total remaining payments and interest over a consistent time horizon, not just the new monthly payment. Extending the term can reduce the payment while increasing the total interest paid. Use lender disclosures and qualified financial advice for a real borrowing decision.
