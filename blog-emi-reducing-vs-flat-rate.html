Reducing-Balance vs Flat-Rate EMI: The Real Cost of a Loan

Two loans can advertise the same interest rate and term yet produce very different payment totals. The reason is often the interest method: reducing-balance interest is calculated on the outstanding principal, while a flat-rate quote applies interest to the original principal throughout the stated term.

Why the interest method matters

With a reducing-balance loan, each payment normally contains interest plus principal. As principal falls, the interest portion falls too. With a flat-rate calculation, the quoted interest is commonly computed from the original principal for the entire term and then spread across the payments.

Reducing-balance EMI formula

EMI = P × r × (1+r)n ÷ ((1+r)n − 1)

P is the initial principal, r is the periodic interest rate and n is the number of payments. If an annual nominal rate is quoted with monthly payments, a simple monthly-rate assumption is annual rate ÷ 12. Always use the lender's stated convention when it differs.

Worked example

Suppose P = 1,000,000, annual rate = 12%, monthly payments and a 5-year term. Using a 1% monthly rate and 60 payments, the reducing-balance EMI is about 22,244. Total scheduled payments are about 1.335 million, so the interest component is about 335,000 before fees, taxes or insurance.

How a flat-rate quote changes the arithmetic

At a flat 12% annual rate for 5 years, simple interest on the original 1,000,000 is 1,000,000 × 0.12 × 5 = 600,000. Spread over 60 months, the payment is 26,666.67. The total is 1.6 million. This does not mean every lender's flat-rate product works exactly this way; the contract controls the calculation.

Do not compare only the advertised percentage

A proper comparison should capture principal, term, payment frequency, interest method, processing fees, insurance, taxes, early-settlement charges and any other compulsory cost. A lower headline rate can still produce a higher total cost if the fee structure or calculation method differs.

What an amortization schedule reveals

An amortization schedule shows the opening balance, interest charged, principal repaid and closing balance for each period. It is useful for checking whether the lender's payment matches the stated method and for understanding how quickly the balance falls.

Common calculation mistakes

  • Using an annual rate as the monthly rate.
  • Comparing flat-rate interest directly with a reducing-balance rate.
  • Ignoring fees and mandatory insurance.
  • Rounding the EMI too early.
  • Assuming every lender uses the same day-count or rate-conversion convention.

When the calculator is most useful

Use a loan calculator to model payment, total interest and outstanding balance under clearly stated assumptions. Then compare those outputs with the lender's official repayment schedule rather than treating a calculator estimate as the contract.

FAQ

Is flat-rate interest the same as reducing-balance interest?

No. They use different principal bases, so the same nominal percentage can produce different payment totals.

Why does reducing-balance interest fall over time?

Because interest is calculated against the remaining principal, which normally decreases after each principal payment.

What should I compare before taking a loan?

Compare total scheduled payments and all compulsory costs, not just the advertised interest rate.
