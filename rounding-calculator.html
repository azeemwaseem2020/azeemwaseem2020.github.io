How rounding works

Choose the place you want to keep, then look at the next digit. In the common decimal rounding rule, a next digit from 5 through 9 increases the kept digit by one; a next digit from 0 through 4 leaves it unchanged.

Rounding example

To round 12.34567 to two decimal places, keep 12.34 and inspect the third decimal digit, 5. The rounded result is 12.35.

Why round numbers?

Rounding makes long decimal results easier to read and communicate. In scientific, financial, construction, and measurement work, keep enough precision for the purpose and avoid rounding intermediate values too early when accuracy matters.

Rounding without losing useful precision

Rounding is mainly a reporting decision. Measurements, prices, percentages and calculated results may require different levels of precision. Keep extra digits during intermediate calculations and round the final reported value unless the problem specifically requires rounding at each step.

Decimal places vs significant figures

Decimal places count digits after the decimal point, while significant figures describe the precision of the measured or calculated number. This calculator rounds by decimal places; it should not be treated as a significant-figures calculator.

Negative numbers

The same digit-checking principle applies to negative values, but software and spreadsheet functions can differ in how they handle certain halfway cases. For regulated, financial or scientific reporting, use the rounding convention required by the relevant standard.

Decimal places are not significant figures

Decimal places count digits to the right of the decimal point. Significant figures communicate the precision of a value. For example, 12.345 rounded to two decimal places is 12.35, while reporting 12.345 to three significant figures gives 12.3. Use the measure required by the assignment, measurement protocol or reporting standard.

Practical rounding guide: examples, precision and reporting

Rounding replaces a number with a nearby value at a chosen place. To round to two decimal places, inspect the third decimal digit: digits 0–4 usually leave the second decimal digit unchanged, while digits 5–9 increase it by one. For example, 12.344 rounded to two decimal places is 12.34; 12.345 rounded using the common half-up rule is 12.35. The selected rule matters when the next digit is exactly 5 or when a system uses a different tie-breaking convention.

Decimal places are not significant figures

Decimal places count digits to the right of the decimal point. Significant figures describe the precision of a measured value, starting at the first non-zero digit. For example, 4.567 rounded to two decimal places is 4.57, while rounded to three significant figures it is also 4.57. But 0.004567 rounded to two decimal places becomes 0.00, whereas three significant figures gives 0.00457. Choose the method that matches the instruction or reporting standard.

Worked checks

  • Money: PKR 245.678 displayed to two decimal places becomes PKR 245.68 under the common half-up rule.
  • Measurement: 3.14159 to three decimal places is 3.142.
  • Whole number: 1,749 rounded to the nearest hundred is 1,700 under the usual nearest-hundred rule because the tens digit is 4.

Avoid rounding errors in multi-step work

Keep extra precision during intermediate calculations and round the final reported value unless the method or regulation explicitly requires rounding at each step. Repeated rounding can create a small cumulative difference. In finance, tax, laboratory work and regulated reporting, follow the exact rule required by the institution or authority; a generic rounding setting may not match every accounting or tax convention.

Rounding also cannot make an uncertain measurement more accurate. If a tape measure or scale only supports a limited precision, reporting many extra decimal places can imply certainty that the source data does not have. Keep the original value when an audit trail is important, state the rounding rule used, and compare results at the same precision when checking two calculations.
