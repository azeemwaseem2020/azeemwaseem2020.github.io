How rounding works

Choose the place you want to keep, then look at the next digit. In the common decimal rounding rule, a next digit from 5 through 9 increases the kept digit by one; a next digit from 0 through 4 leaves it unchanged.

Rounding example

To round 12.34567 to two decimal places, keep 12.34 and inspect the third decimal digit, 5. The rounded result is 12.35.

Why round numbers?

Rounding makes long decimal results easier to read and communicate. In scientific, financial, construction, and measurement work, keep enough precision for the purpose and avoid rounding intermediate values too early when accuracy matters.

FAQ

How many decimal places can I select? This calculator accepts 0 through 15 decimal places.

Does rounding change the original value? No. Rounding creates an approximation for display or reporting; the unrounded value can retain more precision.

Rounding without losing useful precision

Rounding is mainly a reporting decision. Measurements, prices, percentages and calculated results may require different levels of precision. Keep extra digits during intermediate calculations and round the final reported value unless the problem specifically requires rounding at each step.

Decimal places vs significant figures

Decimal places count digits after the decimal point, while significant figures describe the precision of the measured or calculated number. This calculator rounds by decimal places; it should not be treated as a significant-figures calculator.

Negative numbers

The same digit-checking principle applies to negative values, but software and spreadsheet functions can differ in how they handle certain halfway cases. For regulated, financial or scientific reporting, use the rounding convention required by the relevant standard.

Decimal places are not significant figures

Decimal places count digits to the right of the decimal point. Significant figures communicate the precision of a value. For example, 12.345 rounded to two decimal places is 12.35, while reporting 12.345 to three significant figures gives 12.3. Use the measure required by the assignment, measurement protocol or reporting standard.

Best practices

  • Keep extra precision in intermediate calculations and round the final result unless instructions say otherwise.
  • Do not imply a measurement is more precise than the instrument or data supports.
  • Be aware that programming languages and spreadsheets can handle exact halfway cases differently.
  • For financial, laboratory or regulated reporting, follow the required rounding convention.
