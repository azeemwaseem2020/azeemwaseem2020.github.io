Power Factor Calculator

Calculate power factor from real power and apparent power for an AC electrical system.

Power factor formula

For the quantities used here, PF = P ÷ S, where P is real (active) power in watts and S is apparent power in volt-amperes. For a conventional passive load, power factor is normally between 0 and 1.

Worked example

If real power is 800 W and apparent power is 1,000 VA, PF = 800 ÷ 1,000 = 0.80.

Why power factor matters

Power factor describes how effectively apparent power is associated with real power. A lower power factor can mean more current is needed for the same real power, increasing losses and equipment loading in many systems.

Real, reactive and apparent power

In common AC analysis, real power P is measured in watts, reactive power Q in var, and apparent power S in VA. For sinusoidal systems, these quantities are related through the power triangle, while the exact interpretation depends on the load and waveform.

FAQ

Can power factor be greater than 1? For the conventional passive-load definition, no; PF = P/S is normally at most 1.

What does PF = 1 mean? It means real power equals apparent power for the quantities represented by the calculation.

Does this calculator calculate reactive power? No. It calculates PF from real and apparent power.

Understanding power factor

Power factor describes how effectively apparent power is converted into real power in an AC electrical system. For a simple sinusoidal system it is closely related to the phase relationship between voltage and current.

Worked example

If real power is 4 kW and apparent power is 5 kVA, the power factor is 0.80. The result is dimensionless.

Why it matters

A lower power factor can mean more current is required for the same real power, increasing conductor and equipment loading. Actual correction decisions require the characteristics of the electrical system.

Power-factor FAQ

Is power factor measured in watts?

No. Real power is measured in watts, apparent power in volt-amperes, and power factor is their ratio.

Power Factor: Formula, Real Power and Apparent Power

Power factor describes the relationship between real power and apparent power in an AC electrical system. Real power performs useful work or produces heat, light and motion; apparent power describes the combined voltage-current loading. A lower power factor can require more current to deliver the same real power, which may increase conductor losses and equipment loading.

Formula and units

PF = P ÷ S, where P is real power in watts (W) and S is apparent power in volt-amperes (VA). For sinusoidal voltage and current, power factor is also the cosine of the phase angle between them: PF = cos φ. Reactive power Q is measured in vars, and for a balanced sinusoidal model, S² = P² + Q². Distorted waveforms may require a more complete treatment than phase angle alone.

Worked example

If a load consumes 800 W of real power and has apparent power of 1,000 VA, PF = 800 ÷ 1,000 = 0.80. That means real power is 80% of apparent power by this ratio. It does not mean the appliance converts 80% of its input energy into useful output; efficiency and power factor are different quantities.

How to use the calculator

  1. Enter real power in watts, using a reliable meter or equipment specification.
  2. Enter apparent power in volt-amperes, not watt-hours or kilowatt-hours.
  3. Calculate and check that real power does not exceed apparent power for the usual passive-load case.
  4. Interpret the result as a ratio; multiply by 100 only when a percentage-style display is useful.
  5. Confirm whether the data refer to the same operating condition and measurement interval.

Features and practical applications

The calculator helps students, technicians and facility managers interpret electrical measurements and compare loads. It can support basic checks for motors, transformers, lighting and other AC equipment. Improving a poor power factor in an appropriate installation may reduce current for a given real-power demand and release capacity, but any correction equipment must be selected and installed by a qualified professional based on measurements and system requirements.

Assumptions and safety notes

The ratio formula needs compatible real and apparent power measurements. A conventional passive load generally has a power factor between 0 and 1; systems with regeneration or signed power conventions require careful interpretation. A power factor of 1 indicates real power equals apparent power under the chosen definitions, not that the equipment is perfectly efficient. Reactive power does not represent useful net energy transfer over a full ideal AC cycle, but it affects current and network loading.

Do not size capacitors or modify mains wiring using this calculator alone. Real installations can have harmonics, changing loads, phase imbalance and utility-specific requirements. Use manufacturer data, appropriate instruments and a licensed electrician or electrical engineer for corrective design. The calculator is an educational estimate, not a substitute for an electrical safety assessment.

Frequently asked questions

Is power factor the same as efficiency?

No. Power factor relates real power to apparent power; efficiency compares useful output energy or power with input.

Can power factor be improved?

Sometimes, with correctly designed correction equipment or load controls selected for the actual system.

Why is apparent power measured in VA?

It is voltage multiplied by current using the relevant AC definitions, while real power is measured in watts.
