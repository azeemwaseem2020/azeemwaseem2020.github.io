Solar System Sizing: From Appliance Load to Panels, Inverter and Battery

Solar sizing is not one calculation. A useful design separates four questions: how much power can be demanded at once, how much energy is consumed per day, how much battery storage is required, and how much solar generation is needed to replace that energy.

1. Start with load, not panel count

List appliances with their rated power in watts and expected daily operating hours. A simple energy estimate is:

Daily energy (Wh) = Power (W) × Hours per day

For several appliances, calculate each line separately and add the results. This prevents a high-power appliance used briefly from being treated the same as a lower-power appliance running for many hours.

2. Separate peak power from daily energy

Peak demand affects inverter capacity. Daily energy affects the amount of solar generation required. A refrigerator, pump or motor may also have a startup surge above its running wattage. An inverter therefore needs to be checked against both continuous load and relevant surge capability.

3. Estimate inverter capacity

Add the loads that may realistically operate simultaneously, then allow an engineering margin appropriate to the system. Do not simply add every appliance on the property if the operating schedule makes simultaneous use unrealistic. For motor loads, use the equipment specification for starting requirements.

4. Estimate battery storage

Battery sizing depends on desired backup energy, battery chemistry, allowable depth of discharge and system efficiency. A useful planning relationship is:

Nominal battery energy ≈ Required AC energy ÷ (usable fraction × system efficiency)

For example, if a load needs 4 kWh of usable AC energy, the usable battery fraction is 80% and the combined efficiency assumption is 90%, nominal storage is approximately 4 ÷ (0.80 × 0.90) = 5.56 kWh.

5. Convert battery energy to amp-hours when needed

At a nominal battery voltage, a simplified relationship is:

Ah ≈ Wh ÷ V

A 5,560 Wh nominal battery at 48 V is roughly 116 Ah. Real battery specifications, voltage windows, BMS limits and usable capacity should be checked before selecting hardware.

6. Estimate panel capacity

A simplified daily-generation model is:

Solar energy ≈ Panel kW × peak-sun-hours × performance ratio

If daily demand is 8 kWh, peak-sun-hours are 5 and the assumed performance ratio is 0.80, a first-pass array size is 8 ÷ (5 × 0.80) = 2 kW. This is a planning estimate, not a guarantee of production.

7. Account for losses and seasonality

Temperature, dust, shading, wiring, inverter conversion, battery charging and discharging can reduce useful energy. Solar resource also varies by season and location. A design intended to cover critical loads during difficult weather should use a more conservative production assumption than a simple annual-average calculation.

A better design workflow

  1. Inventory loads and operating hours.
  2. Calculate daily Wh and simultaneous peak W.
  3. Identify motor/startup loads.
  4. Define backup duration and critical loads.
  5. Size usable battery energy, then convert to nominal capacity.
  6. Estimate PV size from local solar resource and system losses.
  7. Check inverter PV-input limits, battery voltage compatibility and protection requirements.
  8. Compare the result with actual equipment datasheets before purchase.

What a calculator should and should not decide

A calculator can make arithmetic transparent and expose assumptions. It cannot inspect your roof, predict every shading condition, verify wiring, select protective devices or replace an electrical design review.

FAQ

Should inverter size equal solar-panel size?

Not necessarily. PV array capacity and inverter AC output are different design variables and must be checked against the inverter's allowed PV input and operating range.

How many batteries do I need?

Start from required usable energy, then account for allowable depth of discharge, efficiency, voltage and the battery's rated capacity.

Why is my calculated solar production higher than actual output?

Losses, weather, shading, temperature, soiling and equipment limits can all reduce real production.
