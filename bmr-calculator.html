Basal metabolic rate

Formula & assumptions

Mifflin–St Jeor: BMR = 10W + 6.25H − 5A + S, where S is +5 for men and −161 for women.

Worked example

For a 30-year-old man weighing 70 kg and 175 cm: 10(70) + 6.25(175) − 5(30) + 5 = about 1,649 kcal/day.

Results are estimates; activity, body composition and individual physiology affect actual energy use.

How BMR is estimated

This calculator estimates basal metabolic rate with the Mifflin-St Jeor equation using age, sex, height and weight. BMR is an estimate of the energy the body uses at rest; it is not the same as total daily calorie needs.

Reading the result

The BMR figure provides a starting point for estimating daily energy requirements. Actual energy use varies with activity, body composition, health and other factors, so the number should be treated as an estimate.

Before using it for a diet plan

Use consistent units and accurate measurements. If you are changing your diet for a medical condition, pregnancy, an eating disorder or another health concern, use advice from a qualified health professional.

Understanding the Mifflin–St Jeor estimate

The equation uses weight in kilograms, height in centimetres and age in years. In the version shown here, the constant is +5 for men and −161 for women. For a 30-year-old man weighing 70 kg at 175 cm, the estimate is 10 × 70 + 6.25 × 175 − 5 × 30 + 5, which is approximately 1,649 kcal per day. The result is a predicted resting energy expenditure, not a direct measurement of metabolism.

BMR versus total daily energy needs

Basal metabolic rate refers to energy used to maintain essential functions at rest. Total daily energy expenditure is higher for most people because it also includes movement, exercise, digestion and everyday activity. Therefore, a BMR result should not automatically be treated as a daily calorie target. Activity multipliers and other estimation methods may be used to approximate total needs, but these introduce additional uncertainty.

How to use the estimate responsibly

Enter age, weight and height carefully and keep the units consistent with the fields. Different prediction equations can produce different results, and real energy needs vary with body composition, health, medication, environment and individual physiology. The calculator cannot diagnose a metabolic condition or prescribe a diet. For personalized nutrition guidance—particularly during pregnancy, adolescence, illness, recovery or a history of disordered eating—consult a qualified health professional.

BMR versus daily calorie needs: how to use the estimate

Basal metabolic rate (BMR) estimates the energy your body uses at rest to maintain essential functions. It is not the same as total daily energy expenditure (TDEE), which also includes movement, exercise, digestion and everyday activity. The Mifflin–St Jeor equation is a prediction based on age, height, weight and the sex-specific constant; it is not a direct laboratory measurement of metabolism.

Worked example and next step

For a 30-year-old man weighing 70 kg and measuring 175 cm, the equation gives approximately 1,649 kcal/day. If an illustrative activity multiplier of 1.4 were used, a rough TDEE estimate would be 1,649 × 1.4 ≈ 2,309 kcal/day. The multiplier is only an example: real activity varies, and this page’s BMR result should not be mistaken for a personalized calorie prescription.

What can change the result?

Age, height, weight and the selected equation affect the estimate. Actual energy expenditure can also differ with body composition, health, medications and other individual factors. The calculator assumes the inputs are accurate and that the equation is appropriate for the person being estimated. Use consistent units: weight in kilograms, height in centimetres and age in years.

Responsible use

  • Do not treat BMR as a recommended daily calorie intake; it represents estimated resting energy use.
  • Do not use a single formula to diagnose a metabolic condition or assess nutritional status.
  • For pregnancy, adolescence, eating-disorder recovery, significant illness or medically supervised nutrition, get guidance from a qualified clinician or dietitian.
  • When tracking changes, consider longer-term patterns rather than reacting to small differences between calculator estimates.

Frequently asked questions

Does BMR include exercise?

No. Exercise and everyday movement contribute to total daily energy expenditure, not basal energy use alone.

Is a BMR result exact?

No. It is an equation-based estimate and may differ from measured resting energy expenditure.

Can I use BMR as my daily calorie target?

Not by itself. Daily needs depend on activity and personal context; seek individualized advice when health circumstances make nutrition planning more complex.
