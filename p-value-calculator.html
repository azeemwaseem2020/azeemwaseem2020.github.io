How to interpret a p-value

A p-value measures how compatible an observed result is with a specified null hypothesis. It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true. Use it together with the test statistic, sample size, effect size and study design.

Choosing a significance level

A common threshold is 0.05, but the appropriate significance level should be chosen before interpreting the result and should reflect the consequences of false positives in the particular study.

Example

If a test produces p = 0.03 and the preselected significance level is 0.05, the result is statistically significant under that rule. This does not by itself show that the effect is large, important or practically useful.

What a p-value does not tell you

A p-value does not measure effect size, practical importance, replication probability or the probability that the null hypothesis is true. Confidence intervals and effect-size measures can provide additional context.

P-value FAQ

Is p = 0.05 a universal cutoff?

No. The threshold is a convention, not a law of statistics. It should be selected according to the research context.

Does a small p-value prove causation?

No. Statistical significance alone cannot establish causation; study design and other evidence are required.

P-Value Calculator: What a Two-Sided Normal Approximation Means

This calculator estimates a two-sided p-value from a z statistic using the standard normal distribution. It answers a specific probability question under a statistical model; it does not measure the probability that a hypothesis is true or the size of an effect. The test design and assumptions must be appropriate before interpreting the number.

Concept and formula

For a z statistic under a standard normal null distribution, a two-sided p-value is P(|Z| ≥ |z observed|). It adds the probability in both tails beyond the absolute observed z value. A one-sided test asks a different question and uses only one tail, so its p-value is not generally the same as the two-sided result.

Worked example

If z = 1.96, the two-sided standard-normal p-value is approximately 0.05. This means that, if the null model and assumptions were true, a result at least this far from zero in either direction would occur about 5% of the time. It does not mean there is a 95% probability that the research hypothesis is true, nor does it tell you whether the observed effect is practically meaningful.

How to use the calculator

  1. Enter the z statistic from a test whose reference distribution is standard normal.
  2. Confirm whether the research question requires a one-sided or two-sided test; this tool reports the two-sided result.
  3. Read the approximate p-value and compare it with a significance threshold chosen before analysis.
  4. Report the effect estimate, uncertainty interval, sample size and study design alongside the p-value.

Limitations and good practice

The calculation uses a normal-tail approximation, so its accuracy depends on the underlying statistic and method. It is not a substitute for a t-test, chi-square test, exact test or model-specific p-value when those are required. Avoid interpreting p > 0.05 as proof of no effect or p < 0.05 as proof of causation. Statistical significance and practical importance are different concepts.

Frequently asked questions

Is a p-value the probability the null hypothesis is true?

No. It is a tail probability calculated assuming the null model and test assumptions.

Why is a two-sided p-value often larger?

It accounts for extreme results in both directions rather than one pre-specified tail.

Can I use this for every statistical test?

No. Use it only when a z statistic and standard-normal reference distribution are appropriate.

P-Value Calculator: Tail Direction and Statistical Meaning

A p-value is calculated under a specified null hypothesis and test model. It describes how often a test statistic at least as extreme as the observed one would occur under that model, using the chosen one-tailed or two-tailed definition. It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true and does not measure the size or importance of an effect.

Choose the tail before calculating

A one-tailed test addresses an effect in a prespecified direction; a two-tailed test considers departures in either direction. The correct choice should follow the research question and analysis plan, not whichever option produces a smaller p-value. The calculator's output is only as appropriate as the test statistic and tail setting supplied.

Worked interpretation

If a valid test produces p = 0.03 and the significance threshold was set in advance at 0.05, the result meets that threshold. It does not prove the alternative hypothesis, establish causation, or show that the observed effect is practically meaningful. Report an effect estimate and uncertainty interval where possible.

Common pitfalls

  • Do not interpret p = 0.03 as a 3% probability that the null hypothesis is true.
  • Do not treat statistical significance as a substitute for study quality, sample design or replication.
  • Account for multiple testing and selective reporting when many hypotheses are examined.

For formal analysis, verify that the test statistic matches the intended test and that the sampling assumptions are reasonable. A calculator cannot repair biased data, a misspecified model or an inappropriate study design.

FAQs

Does p below 0.05 prove an effect?

No. It indicates compatibility with a predefined decision rule under the test assumptions.

Can p-values be compared across studies directly?

Not reliably without considering design, sample size, test, effect size and analysis choices.

Should I choose one-tail or two-tail after seeing the result?

No. Choose the direction based on the research question before interpreting the data.
