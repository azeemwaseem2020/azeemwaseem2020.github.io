How to interpret a p-value

A p-value measures how compatible an observed result is with a specified null hypothesis. It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true. Use it together with the test statistic, sample size, effect size and study design.

Choosing a significance level

A common threshold is 0.05, but the appropriate significance level should be chosen before interpreting the result and should reflect the consequences of false positives in the particular study.

Example

If a test produces p = 0.03 and the preselected significance level is 0.05, the result is statistically significant under that rule. This does not by itself show that the effect is large, important or practically useful.

What a p-value does not tell you

A p-value does not measure effect size, practical importance, replication probability or the probability that the null hypothesis is true. Confidence intervals and effect-size measures can provide additional context.

P-value FAQ

Is p = 0.05 a universal cutoff?

No. The threshold is a convention, not a law of statistics. It should be selected according to the research context.

Does a small p-value prove causation?

No. Statistical significance alone cannot establish causation; study design and other evidence are required.
