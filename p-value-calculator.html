Understanding a p-value

A p-value measures how unusual the observed data would be under a specified null hypothesis and statistical model. It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true. The calculation also depends on the test statistic, degrees of freedom and whether the test is one-sided or two-sided.

Worked example

If a valid test produces p = 0.03, a researcher using a pre-specified 0.05 significance threshold would describe the result as statistically significant under that decision rule. The p-value alone does not tell you the size or practical importance of the effect.

Research practice

For experiments, surveys or scientific work in Pakistan or elsewhere, report the effect estimate, uncertainty interval, sample size and test definition alongside the p-value. Do not change a one-sided test to two-sided after seeing the data simply to obtain a preferred conclusion.

FAQs

Does p = 0.03 mean a 3% chance the null is true? No. Is p < 0.05 universally meaningful? No; the threshold is a decision convention. Can a small p-value imply a large effect? No.

P-Value: Interpretation and Limitations

A p-value measures how compatible observed data are with a specified null hypothesis under the statistical model used. A small p-value means the observed result would be relatively unusual if the null hypothesis were true; it does not directly give the probability that the null hypothesis itself is true.

For example, suppose a statistical test produces p = 0.03. If the preselected significance level is 0.05, the result is often described as statistically significant under that decision rule. That statement does not tell you the size or practical importance of the effect. A tiny effect can produce a small p-value with a large sample, while a meaningful effect can fail to reach the threshold with limited data.

In research conducted in Pakistan or elsewhere, the p-value should be reported alongside the estimated effect, confidence interval, sample size and study design. Multiple testing, selective reporting and violated model assumptions can change how the number should be interpreted.

Common mistakes

Do not interpret p = 0.03 as “there is a 3% chance the result happened by chance.” That wording is not generally what a p-value means. Also avoid changing the significance threshold after seeing the data. The hypothesis, test and decision rule should be specified appropriately before analysis.

FAQs

Is p < 0.05 always important? No. Statistical significance and practical importance are different.

Does a p-value prove the alternative hypothesis? No. It is evidence evaluated under a specified null model.

Should I report only the p-value? No. Include effect size and uncertainty where appropriate.
