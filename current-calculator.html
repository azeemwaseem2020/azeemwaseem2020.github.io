Current Calculator

Calculate electrical current from voltage and resistance using Ohm's law.

Current formula

For a simple resistive circuit, Ohm's law gives I = V ÷ R, where I is current in amperes, V is voltage in volts, and R is resistance in ohms.

Worked example

For 12 V across a 6 Ω resistive load, I = 12 ÷ 6 = 2 A.

Current, voltage and power

Current is related to power by P = VI in the simple DC or unity-power-factor case. AC loads may require power factor, impedance, and phase information, so a basic Ohm's-law calculation is not a complete model for every circuit.

What can change real current?

Component tolerance, temperature, changing loads, source voltage, wiring resistance and circuit design can make measured current differ from an ideal calculation.

FAQ

Can I calculate current from watts? Yes, if voltage is also known; AC real-power calculations may additionally require power factor and phase information.

What unit is current measured in? Current is measured in amperes (A), commonly called amps.

What happens if resistance is zero? The ideal formula would divide by zero, so this calculator rejects zero resistance rather than implying an infinite real-world current.

Electric current in circuit calculations

Electric current describes the rate at which electric charge passes a point in a circuit. Its SI unit is the ampere, equivalent to coulombs per second.

Worked example

If 20 coulombs of charge pass a point in 4 seconds, the average current is 5 A.

DC and changing current

The simple calculation gives average current over an interval. Alternating or rapidly changing circuits may require instantaneous or RMS quantities depending on the application.

Current FAQ

Is current the same as voltage?

No. Current is charge flow rate, while voltage represents electric potential difference.

Electric current calculator guide: Ohm's law and circuit assumptions

Electric current is the rate at which electric charge passes a point in a circuit. Its SI unit is the ampere (A), and one ampere corresponds to one coulomb of charge per second. In a simple resistive circuit, Ohm's law relates current, voltage and resistance: I = V ÷ R. If a 12 V supply is connected across a 6 Ω resistor, the current is 12 ÷ 6 = 2 A under the ideal assumptions of the calculation.

Check the units before calculating

Voltage should be entered in volts and resistance in ohms for the formula to return amperes. If resistance is given in kilohms, convert it to ohms first: 2 kΩ equals 2,000 Ω. With 12 V across 2,000 Ω, current is 0.006 A, or 6 mA. Confusing milliamps with amps creates a factor-of-1,000 error.

Limits of the simple model

The relationship I = V/R describes an ohmic resistance under conditions where the resistance is suitably constant. Real components may heat up, vary with voltage or behave nonlinearly. In AC circuits, voltage and current can be time-varying, and RMS values, impedance and phase relationships may be needed. Do not treat a simple DC calculation as a complete design for a mains-powered circuit.

Electrical safety

Current depends on the full circuit path and the source, not just a single number entered into a calculator. This page is for learning and basic estimates; it does not determine whether a circuit, wire, fuse or protective device is safe. For mains wiring or equipment selection, follow local electrical codes and consult a qualified electrician. Never test a live circuit based only on a calculator result.

For a first-pass estimate, calculate the expected current and then compare it with the component specifications and applicable electrical standards; never use the estimate alone to choose a wire or protection rating.
