Cash Flow Calculator

Calculate net cash flow for a period from total cash inflows and cash outflows.

Net cash flow formula

Net cash flow = total cash inflows − total cash outflows. A positive result means more cash entered than left during the selected period.

Worked example

If monthly inflows are $25,000 and outflows are $19,000, net cash flow is $6,000.

Cash flow is not profit

Cash movement and accounting profit are different concepts. Loans, asset sales, receivables, inventory and non-cash expenses can make cash flow differ substantially from reported profit.

FAQ

What does negative cash flow mean? It means outflows exceeded inflows during the selected period.

Should starting cash be included? Starting cash is separate from net cash flow; adding it gives the ending cash balance when no other adjustments apply.

Calculation context

Cash-flow analysis compares money entering and leaving a business or project over a defined period. Separating operating, investing and financing assumptions can make the resulting cash-flow picture easier to interpret.

Reading a cash-flow estimate

Cash flow tracks money entering and leaving a business or project. A positive period balance does not necessarily mean the business is profitable, because profit and cash flow use different accounting concepts.

Separate operating and one-off items

When building a practical estimate, distinguish recurring operating receipts and payments from financing, capital purchases and unusual one-time items. This makes the result easier to interpret and update.

Use a consistent period

Monthly cash-flow planning should use monthly inputs wherever possible. Large annual totals can hide timing gaps that create short-term cash shortages.

Cash-flow FAQ

Can a profitable business have negative cash flow?

Yes. Timing differences, inventory purchases, debt payments or major capital spending can reduce cash even when reported profit is positive.

Separate cash-flow categories

For a more useful business estimate, group cash movements into operating, investing and financing activities. Operating cash movements can include customer receipts, supplier payments, wages and rent. Investing movements may include buying or selling equipment, while financing movements can include loan proceeds, principal repayments or owner contributions. These categories help explain why a period's net cash flow changed; they do not all represent sales revenue or operating profit.

Example: net flow versus ending balance

If a business begins the month with $8,000 in cash, receives $25,000 and pays $19,000 during the month, net cash flow is $6,000 and the simplified ending cash balance is $14,000. The calculator reports the period's net movement, not the opening or closing balance. Include opening cash separately when preparing a cash forecast.

Match timing as well as totals

A monthly total can look healthy even when bills fall due before customers pay. For cash planning, record expected receipt and payment dates and review shorter intervals when the business has tight working capital. Use realistic collection times, payroll dates, tax deadlines, supplier terms and seasonal changes rather than assuming all money arrives evenly.

Use a cash-flow estimate responsibly

Enter inflows and outflows for the same time period and currency. Avoid counting the same transaction twice, and distinguish cash received from invoices issued: sales made on credit may not bring cash in immediately. Likewise, loan principal affects cash but is not an ordinary operating expense in the same way as interest. This simple calculator summarizes the figures you enter; it does not classify transactions or produce a full cash-flow statement.

Keep inflows and outflows in the same period

Net cash flow is cash received minus cash paid during the period being analysed. Do not combine monthly inflows with annual outflows unless you first convert them to the same period. A positive result means more cash came in than went out for the selected period; it does not automatically mean the business was profitable.

Cash flow versus accounting profit

Profit can include revenue that has not yet been collected and expenses that have not yet been paid. Cash flow follows the timing of cash movements, so a profitable business can still face a cash shortfall when customers pay late or large bills fall due before receipts arrive.

Separate operating, investing and financing activity

Operating cash flow relates to normal business operations; investing cash flow includes purchases or sales of long-term assets; financing cash flow includes borrowing, repayments and owner funding. For a useful view, record each category separately before calculating the total change in cash.

Use the result for planning, not as a complete forecast

A single-period calculation does not model seasonality, payment delays or future obligations. Build a dated cash-flow forecast and test a downside scenario if the result will guide payroll, inventory or investment decisions.
