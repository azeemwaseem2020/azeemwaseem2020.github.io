Present value in practical terms

Present value converts a future cash amount into an equivalent value today under a chosen discount or interest rate. The calculation is useful for comparing cash flows that occur at different times. It does not tell you which discount rate is appropriate; that rate depends on the financial situation and risk being evaluated.

Formula

PV = FV ÷ (1+r)^n for annual compounding, where FV is future value, r is the periodic rate and n is the number of periods.

Worked example

At a 10% annual discount rate, Rs 121,000 received in two years has PV = 121,000 ÷ 1.10² = Rs 100,000. The result changes if the discount rate or compounding period changes.

Pakistan-specific example

A business in Pakistan comparing a payment received today with a promised payment later can use present value as a planning measure, then separately consider inflation, default risk, taxes and financing costs.

Common questions

Does PV predict inflation? No. Inflation is only one possible component of a discount assumption.

Can rates be monthly? Yes, when the number of periods and rate are both monthly.

Is PV the same as today's market price? Not necessarily.

Why does a higher rate lower PV? A larger discount reduces the present equivalent of the same future amount.

For multiple future payments, calculate the present value of each cash flow separately and add them. A payment received sooner generally has a larger present value than the same payment received later when the discount rate is positive. This is why timing matters in financial comparisons.

When comparing real financial products, document the chosen rate, compounding convention and timing of each payment. A mathematically precise result can still be misleading if those assumptions do not match the actual contract.
