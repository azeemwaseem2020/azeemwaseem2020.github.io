US HOUSEHOLD ENERGY GUIDE

How to Read a US Electricity Bill: kWh, Rates and Extra Charges

To estimate an electric bill accurately, start with the billing period and energy usage, then identify which per-kWh charges and fixed fees apply. The amount due alone rarely tells you what each appliance costs to run.

Written and maintained by Abdul Qadir · Calculation methodology

1. Find your electricity usage in kWh

Look for a line such as “current usage,” “electricity used,” or “kWh.” A kilowatt-hour (kWh) measures energy, not power. If the bill says you used 900 kWh during a 30-day billing period, use 900 as the monthly usage input in a bill estimator. If the period is 27 or 34 days, remember that it is not a standard month; compare bills using kWh per day as well as the total.

If your utility uses estimated meter readings, a later actual reading can cause an adjustment. Check whether the bill marks the reading as actual or estimated before treating a month-to-month change as a change in household consumption.

2. Understand cents per kWh

Your rate may appear as a price per kWh, a supply or generation charge, or a set of charges that must be divided by usage to create an approximate blended rate. Convert cents to dollars before multiplying: 16 cents per kWh is $0.16 per kWh. For 900 kWh, the energy charge at that rate is 900 × $0.16 = $144.

Do not assume the first price shown is the full price of electricity. In some markets, the bill separates the energy supply from delivery or distribution. Other plans combine items or use several rate components. Read the charge labels and your utility’s rate schedule to understand what is included.

3. Separate per-kWh charges from fixed fees

A monthly customer charge or basic service fee may apply even when usage is low. Delivery charges, public-purpose fees, taxes, riders, credits and other adjustments can also affect the final amount. A useful estimate separates costs into three groups:

  • Usage-based charges: charges that rise with each kWh used.
  • Fixed charges: recurring amounts that do not directly depend on monthly kWh.
  • Other bill items: taxes, adjustments, credits or fees that apply to that bill.

Check which components your entered rate already includes. If your rate is an all-in blended rate derived from the bill, adding the same delivery charge again will overestimate the total. If you enter only the supply rate, the estimate will understate the bill unless you also include the omitted charges.

4. Check whether your plan uses tiers or time-of-use pricing

A single-rate estimate is simplest, but not every plan charges the same price for every kWh. A tiered plan can apply one rate to an initial usage block and another rate to later blocks. A time-of-use plan can charge different rates during peak, off-peak or shoulder hours. Some bills also include demand charges or seasonal rules.

For a simple one-rate estimate, use the US Electricity Bill Calculator. For block-based tier pricing, use the Tiered Electricity Rate Calculator. To estimate the energy cost of an appliance from watts, runtime and a rate, use the Electricity Cost Calculator. These tools use different inputs because a household bill and an appliance’s running cost are different questions.

5. Worked example: estimate a monthly bill

Suppose a household used 900 kWh, enters a single rate of 16 cents per kWh, and has $15 in fixed charges plus $4 in other charges. The usage charge is 900 × $0.16 = $144. Add $15 and $4 to get an estimated total of $163. This is a worked example, not a current price recommendation or a statement of what a typical household pays.

If your actual bill is $190, do not automatically conclude that the calculator is wrong or that the utility overcharged you. Compare the billing dates, kWh, rate structure, delivery components, taxes, credits and any prior-period adjustment. The estimate only includes what you enter and the pricing model it supports.

6. Use official average-price data carefully

The US Energy Information Administration (EIA) publishes residential electricity price statistics by state and nationally. These data are useful for broad context and comparisons, but state averages blend customers and utilities with different plans. They are not a quote for your address and may not match the latest rate change on your account. Start with your own bill when estimating your actual cost.

Reference: EIA Electric Power Annual — state electricity price data. Check your utility’s current tariff for the exact plan and billing period.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Entering dollars as cents, such as entering 0.16 when the field expects 16 cents.
  • Using the amount due without checking usage and the length of the billing period.
  • Counting delivery charges twice after entering an all-in rate.
  • Using one flat rate for a tiered or time-of-use plan without noting the limitation.
  • Assuming a national or state average is the exact price charged by your utility.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate an electric bill from kWh?

For a single-rate estimate, multiply kWh by the dollar-per-kWh rate, then add fixed fees and other charges not already included in that rate.

What is the difference between supply and delivery charges?

Supply generally refers to the electricity commodity or generation, while delivery covers the network and service that brings electricity to the property. Bill labels and market rules vary, so check your utility’s explanation.

Can I calculate my exact bill with an online calculator?

Only if the calculator models all relevant details and you enter the correct tariff and bill inputs. A simplified calculator provides an estimate, not a guarantee of the utility’s final amount.

Important: This guide is educational. Your utility bill and current tariff are the authoritative sources for your actual charges.
