Solar Load Calculator Guide: How to Size a Solar System

Before deciding how many panels you need, work out both the power your appliances may draw at once and the energy they use over a typical day.

Quick answer: estimate appliance energy with watts × hours ÷ 1,000, total the daily kWh, then use peak sun hours and an efficiency assumption for a first-pass solar size.

Watts, kW and kWh

Watts describe power. Kilowatts are watts divided by 1,000. Kilowatt-hours describe energy used over time. For a simple appliance estimate, watts × hours ÷ 1,000 = kWh.

Worked example

A 1,000-watt appliance running for 5 hours uses about 5 kWh. If another appliance uses 600 watts for 4 hours, it adds about 2.4 kWh. Together those two loads account for roughly 7.4 kWh for the day.

How a first-pass solar estimate works

A common planning relationship is solar system size (kW) = daily energy demand ÷ (peak sun hours × system efficiency). For example, 10 kWh of daily demand with an assumed 5 peak-sun-hour equivalent and 80% overall efficiency gives 10 ÷ (5 × 0.8) = 2.5 kW. The result is only a planning estimate because real generation changes with weather, shading, temperature and system design.

Peak load vs daily energy

These are different questions. Peak load helps with inverter capacity and whether several appliances can run together. Daily energy helps estimate how much solar generation is needed over a day. A system can have enough daily energy on paper but still need a larger inverter for short periods of high demand.

What can change the estimate?

Appliance demand

High-power appliances such as pumps and air conditioners can materially change peak load.

Usage pattern

Two homes with the same appliances can have different daily energy use because their hours of operation differ.

Site conditions

Shading, roof direction, season, temperature, dust and equipment losses affect actual generation.

Planning a solar system in Pakistan

For a Pakistani home, look at recent electricity bills and typical daytime and evening usage rather than relying on a single appliance list. Summer air-conditioning can substantially increase both peak demand and daily consumption. Local roof conditions and the intended battery or grid setup also matter.

Before buying equipment

  1. Record the appliances you actually use.
  2. Estimate realistic daily operating hours.
  3. Separate peak load from total daily energy.
  4. Check shading and roof space.
  5. Use the calculator as a first estimate, then have the final design checked for inverter, battery, wiring, protection and installation requirements.

Important limitation

A calculator cannot perform a site survey or guarantee solar production. Actual output depends on equipment specifications, weather and installation conditions. Treat the result as a planning estimate rather than a final engineering design.

Frequently asked questions

What does this calculator help estimate?

It applies the stated inputs and standard formula to produce an estimate. The result should be checked against the equipment specification, local conditions and the assumptions used.

Are the results exact?

No calculation is exact when real-world inputs vary. Treat the result as a planning estimate and verify important decisions with current technical specifications.

Which inputs should I check first?

Check the units, rated values, system conditions and any losses or efficiency assumptions before relying on the final result.
