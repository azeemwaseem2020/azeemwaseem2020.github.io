Projectile Motion Calculator — Range, Maximum Height & Flight Time

Solve ideal projectile motion from launch speed and angle when launch and landing heights are equal.

Formulas

  • Range: R = v₀² sin(2θ) ÷ g
  • Maximum height: H = v₀² sin²θ ÷ (2g)
  • Flight time: T = 2v₀ sinθ ÷ g

Worked example

For v₀ = 20 m/s and θ = 45°, the ideal range is about 40.77 m using g = 9.80665 m/s².

Assumptions

Launch and landing heights are equal, acceleration is constant downward, and air resistance, wind and spin are ignored. For a different landing height, use a full projectile-motion model rather than these simplified equations.

Projectile Motion Calculator Guide — Range, Height & Flight Time

Use this calculator for ideal projectile-motion problems where launch and landing heights are equal and air resistance is ignored.

Formulas

  • Range: R = v₀² sin(2θ) / g
  • Maximum height: H = v₀² sin²θ / (2g)
  • Flight time: T = 2v₀ sinθ / g

Worked example

For v₀ = 20 m/s, θ = 45°, and g = 9.80665 m/s², the ideal range is about 40.77 m.

Why launch angle matters

In the equal-height, no-air-resistance model, complementary launch angles can produce the same ideal range. The familiar 45° maximum-range result applies only under these simplified conditions.

Study workflow

  1. Check the launch speed, angle and gravity value.
  2. Confirm that launch and landing heights are equal.
  3. Compare the displayed range, height and time with the formulas.
  4. Keep units consistent when using the result elsewhere.

Limitations

Real projectiles can be affected by air resistance, wind, spin, shape and a different landing height. Those factors can make the ideal equations inaccurate.

Read Calcora calculation methodology

Projectile motion assumptions

Ideal projectile motion separates horizontal and vertical motion while treating gravitational acceleration as constant and neglecting air resistance. The initial speed and launch angle determine the components of velocity.

Worked example

For a launch speed of 20 m/s at 30° above horizontal, the ideal initial horizontal component is about 17.32 m/s and the vertical component is 10 m/s.

Real-world limits

Air drag, wind, changing terrain and launch or landing height can materially change a real trajectory. The ideal equations should therefore be treated as a model, not a guarantee of field performance.

Projectile FAQ

Does projectile motion have constant acceleration?

In the ideal model near Earth's surface, vertical acceleration is approximately constant downward while horizontal acceleration is zero.
