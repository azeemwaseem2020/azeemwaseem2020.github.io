Force Calculator — F = ma

Calculate force from mass and acceleration using Newton's second law and see the formula, units and calculation steps.

Calculate force from mass and acceleration

Tip: For a signed force/acceleration problem, enter the sign that represents your chosen direction. For magnitude-only problems, use positive values.

Force formula

F = m × a, where F is force in newtons (N), m is mass in kilograms (kg), and a is acceleration in metres per second squared (m/s²).

How to calculate force

  1. Enter the mass in kilograms.
  2. Enter the acceleration in m/s².
  3. Multiply mass by acceleration.
  4. Report the result in newtons.

Worked example

For a 10 kg object accelerating at 2 m/s², F = 10 × 2 = 20 N.

Understanding newtons

One newton is the force required to accelerate a 1 kg mass at 1 m/s². The unit follows directly from the relationship N = kg·m/s².

Mass, acceleration and force

For a fixed acceleration, increasing mass increases the required net force proportionally. For a fixed mass, increasing acceleration also increases force proportionally.

Common mistakes

  • Using grams instead of kilograms without converting the mass.
  • Using speed or velocity where acceleration is required.
  • Mixing incompatible units.
  • Confusing net force with an individual force when several forces act on an object.

When to use this calculator

This calculator is useful for introductory physics, mechanics, classroom exercises and simple motion problems where mass and acceleration are known. It can also be used as a quick newton calculator when you want to convert a mass-and-acceleration situation into force.

Force, weight and net force are not the same question

Force is measured in newtons. Weight is the gravitational force acting on a mass, so near Earth it is approximately W = mg. Net force is the vector sum of all forces acting on an object. If friction, drag or another opposing force is present, do not treat the applied force alone as the net force.

Important limitation

This is a basic Newtonian calculation of net force from mass and acceleration. It does not model friction, drag, changing mass, relativistic effects or complex systems with multiple forces.

Force Calculator Guide — F = ma

Use this calculator to find the net force from mass and acceleration. It can also help check a physics exercise when the quantities are already expressed in compatible units.

Formula

F = ma, where F is net force in newtons, m is mass in kilograms, and a is acceleration in metres per second squared.

Worked example

For a 10 kg object accelerating at 3 m/s², the net force magnitude is 30 N.

Net force versus one applied force

Newton's second law applies to the net force. If several forces act on an object, add their signed or vector components first; the result is not necessarily equal to any single applied force.

Unit conversions

Convert grams to kilograms and other acceleration units to m/s² before applying F = ma. The calculator can handle supported unit choices, but checking the units is still important when interpreting the result.

Direction matters

Force is a vector. This page's basic result represents a magnitude; a complete mechanics problem may also require the direction of the net force.

Limits

F = ma is the standard classical mechanics relationship for the stated reference frame and conditions. More advanced problems may require a full vector treatment or relativistic mechanics.

FORCE MODEL CHECK

F = ma describes net force, not automatically every individual force

Newton's second law relates net force to mass and acceleration. In a real problem, several forces may act at once: gravity, friction, tension, normal force or an applied force. The calculator should therefore be used with a clear definition of which force the inputs represent.

Net force

If the object accelerates, Fnet = ma describes the vector sum of the forces.

Direction matters

Choose a sign convention when forces or acceleration point in opposite directions. Magnitude alone can hide the direction of the result.

Free-body check

For multi-force problems, identify the forces first and then use the net result rather than treating every force as ma.
