Amps to watts formula

For a basic DC circuit or unity-power-factor case, P = V × I. For single-phase AC real power, a simplified relationship is P = V × I × PF. Three-phase systems require the appropriate three-phase relationship.

Worked examples

At 230 V and 5 A with unity power factor, power is 1,150 W. At the same voltage and current with PF 0.8, real power is about 920 W in the simplified single-phase model.

Important limitation

Current alone cannot determine watts. Voltage is required, and many AC loads also require power factor and system configuration. Use appropriate electrical safety and engineering practices for mains installations.

FAQ

Is voltage required to calculate watts?

Yes. Current alone is not enough to determine power because watts depend on voltage; AC real-power estimates may also require power factor and phase information.

Why can appliance watts differ from volts × amps?

AC loads can have power factor below 1 and operating current can vary.

How many watts is 1 amp?

It depends on voltage. At 12 V, 1 A is 12 W in a DC/unity-PF case; at 230 V, it is 230 W.

Calculating watts from current and voltage

Electrical power depends on current, voltage and the circuit's power factor. For a DC circuit, watts = volts × amps. For single-phase AC, real power can be estimated as volts × amps × power factor. Three-phase systems require the appropriate three-phase relationship.

Worked example

At 230 V and 5 A in a DC or unity-power-factor example, power is 1,150 W. If the power factor is below 1 in an AC load, real power is lower than the simple volts × amps apparent-power figure.

Amps to watts FAQ

Can I convert amps to watts without voltage?

No. Voltage is required, and AC real-power calculations may also require power factor and phase information.

Why does an appliance rating differ from the calculation?

Appliances can have changing loads, power-factor effects, starting current and manufacturer-specific operating conditions.

Real power, apparent power and power factor

For DC, electrical power is P = V × I. In a single-phase AC circuit, real power is P = V × I × PF, where PF is the power factor. The product V × I without the power factor is apparent power, measured in volt-amperes (VA), rather than necessarily real power in watts. For a balanced three-phase AC system, real power is commonly calculated as √3 × line voltage × line current × power factor. Use the relationship that matches the circuit rather than applying the single-phase formula to every installation.

Example using a household supply

At 230 volts and 5 amps, the apparent power is 1,150 VA. If a single-phase AC load has a power factor of 0.8, estimated real power is 230 × 5 × 0.8 = 920 W. For a DC load, or an AC load with power factor close to 1, the simple voltage-times-current calculation gives a similar watt value. Actual appliance consumption can vary with operating conditions.

Safety and interpretation

This calculator is for estimating electrical power, not for choosing cable sizes, circuit breakers or protective devices. Those choices depend on local electrical standards, installation conditions, load characteristics and qualified assessment. Check an appliance nameplate when available, and do not assume that a calculated value represents starting current or continuous demand. If the phase type or power factor is unknown, treat the result as an estimate and verify the system details before using it for equipment decisions.
