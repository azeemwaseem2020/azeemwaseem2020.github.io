Separate your deposits from estimated growth

A useful projection should show how much you put in and how much of the final balance comes from the assumed rate.

Important: a selected return is an assumption, not a guaranteed investment result. Fees, tax, inflation and changing returns can materially affect real outcomes.

What this calculator can tell you

Calcora estimates a future balance from an initial amount plus regular end-of-period contributions. It also separates total contributions from estimated growth so you can understand what is driving the final number.

Compound interest formula

For an initial amount without contributions, A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt). With a contribution at the end of each compounding period, the contribution component is C × ((1 + r/n)^(nt) − 1) ÷ (r/n). If the rate is zero, the result is simply the starting amount plus contributions.

Worked example

Imagine starting with 10,000 units of currency, adding nothing during the period, using an assumed 8% annual rate compounded monthly for 10 years. The calculator applies the stated formula to produce a mathematical projection. The result is not a promise that an actual bank account or investment will earn 8% every year.

Compound interest with monthly deposits

When compounds per year is set to 12, the calculator treats one regular contribution as occurring at the end of each month. That timing matters: a deposit made at the beginning of a period has more time to earn growth than a deposit made at the end.

Why time can matter so much

Compounding allows previously accumulated growth to participate in later growth. This means a longer horizon can make the assumed rate more influential, but the effect is entirely dependent on the rate and contribution pattern you enter.

Starting balance versus regular saving

Two savers can reach similar projected balances using different strategies. One may start with more capital while another contributes regularly. Looking at total contributions separately from estimated growth helps you see that difference.

How changing the assumptions changes the projection

  • Higher starting amount: gives the assumed rate more capital to compound.
  • Larger regular contribution: increases the amount you personally add over time.
  • Higher assumed rate: increases mathematical growth but also makes the projection more sensitive to an assumption that may not be achieved.
  • Longer period: provides more compounding periods.
  • Compounding frequency: can change the mathematical result for the same nominal rate.

Inflation, taxes and fees

A future nominal balance is not the same as future purchasing power. Inflation can reduce real buying power, while taxes and fees can reduce net growth. If you have reliable product-specific figures, include them in a separate analysis rather than assuming one universal inflation or tax rate.

Understanding compound growth

Use the calculator to separate your starting balance, regular contributions and estimated growth. That makes it easier to test different assumptions without treating a projected return as a guarantee.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing an annual nominal rate with an effective annual rate.
  • Assuming more frequent compounding always creates a large difference.
  • Forgetting whether deposits occur at the beginning or end of each period.
  • Treating a constant assumed return as a guaranteed real-world return.
  • Ignoring taxes, fees or inflation when evaluating purchasing power.

Frequently asked questions

What is compound interest?

It is growth calculated on an initial amount plus previously accumulated growth, according to the product's compounding rules.

Does this calculator guarantee investment returns?

No. It produces a mathematical projection from your assumptions. Actual returns can vary, and some investments can lose value.

Can I add monthly deposits?

Yes. Set the contribution to the amount added per compounding period and use 12 compounds per year for a monthly end-of-period contribution scenario.

Does it account for inflation?

No. The displayed future value is nominal. Consider inflation separately when you want to estimate future purchasing power.

What this calculator does not promise

  • It does not predict market returns.
  • It does not include every tax, fee or inflation effect.
  • It does not model irregular deposits or withdrawals.
  • It does not replace a bank or investment provider's official calculation.

How compound interest grows

Compound interest means previously credited interest becomes part of the balance used for later interest calculations. For a single initial deposit with a nominal annual rate r compounded n times per year, the future value is A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt).

Worked example

For $1,000 at a 6% nominal annual rate compounded monthly for 2 years, the formula uses P = 1,000, r = 0.06, n = 12 and t = 2. The result is higher than simple interest because interest is credited throughout the year and subsequently compounds.

Compound interest FAQ

Does compounding frequency matter?

Yes. For the same positive nominal rate, more frequent compounding generally produces a slightly higher effective annual return.

Does this include regular deposits?

The basic compound-interest formula does not automatically represent a stream of deposits. Recurring contributions require a future-value model that accounts for each contribution.
