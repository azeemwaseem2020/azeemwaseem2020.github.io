Electrical Power Calculator

Calculate electrical power, voltage, or current using the standard relationship P = V × I.

Electrical power formulas

The basic relationship is P = V × I. Rearranging it gives V = P ÷ I and I = P ÷ V. For a resistive load, related forms include P = I²R and P = V²/R.

Worked examples

Power: 12 V × 10 A = 120 W.

Current: a 240 W load at 12 V draws 20 A in the simplified model.

Voltage: a 120 W load drawing 10 A has 12 V across it.

Watts, volts and amps

Voltage is electrical potential difference, current is charge flow, and power is the rate of electrical energy transfer. Keep SI units consistent when applying these equations.

DC and AC limitations

This calculator is most straightforward for DC or simplified resistive calculations. AC systems can require RMS values, power factor, phase information, and system configuration. For real electrical installations, use appropriate engineering methods and safety procedures.

Electrical power FAQ

How do I calculate watts from volts and amps?

Multiply voltage by current: P = V × I.

Can I calculate amps from watts?

Yes, if voltage is known: I = P ÷ V. Real AC systems may require power factor and other details.

Is electrical power the same as energy?

No. Power is a rate, measured in watts. Energy is power accumulated over time and may be measured in watt-hours or joules.

Calculation context

Electrical power calculations connect voltage, current and resistance. For a basic resistive model, P = VI and equivalent forms can be used when the required quantities are known.

Electrical power examples: watts, volts and amps

For a simple resistive load, power is voltage multiplied by current: P = V × I. A device operating at 12 V and drawing 2 A uses 24 W of electrical power at that operating point. Rearranging the same relationship gives I = P ÷ V, so a 60 W load at 12 V draws 5 A in the idealized model.

AC power factor matters

For AC circuits, multiplying RMS voltage by RMS current gives apparent power in volt-amperes (VA). Real power in watts can be lower when the power factor is below 1; for a single-phase sinusoidal circuit, P = V × I × power factor. Motors and other reactive or non-linear loads may need more careful treatment than a basic DC or resistive calculation.

Use ratings safely

Calculated current is not, by itself, a wire-size or breaker recommendation. Cable length, installation method, ambient temperature, insulation, voltage drop and local electrical codes all matter. Confirm equipment ratings and consult a qualified electrician for installation or protection decisions.
