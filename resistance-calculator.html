Resistance Calculator

Calculate resistance from voltage and current using Ohm's law.

Resistance formula

Ohm's law gives R = V ÷ I, where R is resistance in ohms, V is voltage in volts, and I is current in amperes.

Worked example

With 12 V and 3 A, R = 12 ÷ 3 = 4 Ω.

Units and practical limits

Use volts and amperes for the SI form. A real component may not have constant resistance: temperature, material properties, frequency, and operating point can affect electrical behavior.

Related Ohm's law formulas

Voltage can be calculated as V = IR and current as I = V/R. These relationships describe an ohmic model; more complex components may require impedance or nonlinear models.

FAQ

What is resistance measured in? Resistance is measured in ohms (Ω).

Can current be zero? If current is exactly zero, R = V/I is undefined, so another method or circuit interpretation is needed.

Can resistance be negative? Ordinary passive resistors have positive resistance; specialized active circuits can exhibit different behavior and are outside this simple calculator.

Interpreting resistance

Resistance describes how strongly a component opposes electric current. Under the simple Ohm's law model, resistance is the ratio of voltage to current. A higher resistance at the same voltage produces lower current.

Example

A component with 12 V across it and 2 A through it has a resistance of 6 Ω. If the component heats up and its resistance changes, the simple calculation using the original values may no longer describe its operating condition accurately.

Practical limits

Real components can have temperature coefficients, tolerances and nonlinear behaviour. For circuit design, also check the component's rated power because a mathematically valid resistance value does not by itself prove that the component can safely dissipate the resulting heat.

Check the circuit assumptions

Ohm’s law gives resistance as voltage divided by current for the measured operating point. For example, 9 V across a component carrying 0.5 A gives 18 Ω. Use the voltage across the component, not automatically the power-supply rating, and measure current through the same component.

Common sources of error

  • Entering milliamperes as amperes without converting: 500 mA = 0.5 A.
  • Using supply voltage when other components cause a voltage drop.
  • Assuming a lamp, diode or other nonlinear component has constant resistance at every operating point.
  • Ignoring resistor tolerance, temperature and rated power in a design.

If current is zero, the ratio V/I is undefined. A negative result can reflect measurement polarity or an active circuit and should be interpreted in context. This tool calculates a ratio; it does not confirm that a component or circuit is safe.

Check the relationship with Ohm’s law

For a component that follows Ohm’s law under the stated operating conditions, resistance is voltage divided by current: R = V ÷ I. If a 12 V supply drives 2 A through a load, the calculated resistance is 6 Ω. The input voltage and current must describe the same operating point.

What if current is zero?

Division by zero is undefined, so a resistance value cannot be calculated from a zero-current input using this formula. An open circuit, a disconnected load and a real component at very low current may require different diagnostic interpretations; the equation alone cannot identify the cause.

Practical measurement cautions

Resistance can change with temperature and operating conditions. For many electronic components, the resistance inferred from V/I may be an effective operating-point value rather than a fixed material property. Disconnect power and follow instrument guidance before measuring resistance directly with a multimeter.

Keep electrical units consistent

Use volts for voltage and amperes for current to obtain ohms. If the source uses milliamps, convert to amperes first: 500 mA = 0.5 A. Confusing milliamps with amps changes the result by a factor of 1,000.
