Mass–Energy Calculator Guide

Use this calculator to find the rest-energy equivalent of a mass, or the equivalent mass associated with a known amount of energy.

Formula

E = mc², where E is energy in joules, m is mass in kilograms, and c is the speed of light in vacuum: 299,792,458 metres per second.

Worked example

A mass of 1 kg has a rest-energy equivalent of approximately 8.99 × 1016 J. The result scales directly with mass.

Finding mass from energy

Rearrange the relationship to m = E/c² when energy is known. Keep mass in kilograms and energy in joules for the SI result.

What the result means

This is a rest-energy equivalence, not a prediction that an ordinary object will release all of that energy under normal conditions. The amount of energy actually released depends on the physical process involved.

Limits

This tool is intended for introductory calculations. It does not model kinetic energy, nuclear reaction products, binding energy, or a complete relativistic system.

Mass-energy equivalence

Mass-energy equivalence expresses the relationship between mass and energy through the square of the speed of light. Because that speed is very large, even a small mass corresponds to a very large energy value.

Worked example

One gram of mass corresponds to about 8.99 × 10¹³ joules under complete mass-energy conversion.

Interpret the result carefully

The equation gives equivalence, not a claim that ordinary objects can readily convert all their rest mass into usable energy. Physical processes determine how much energy is actually released.

Mass-energy FAQ

Is all mass normally converted into energy?

No. Complete conversion is an ideal equivalence; ordinary chemical and mechanical processes involve far smaller energy changes.

Mass–energy equivalence: units, scale and what E = mc² does not claim

The relation E = mc² gives the rest-energy equivalent of mass m, with c representing the speed of light in vacuum (about 299,792,458 m/s). Because the speed of light is squared, a small mass corresponds to a very large energy in joules. Keep mass in kilograms if using c in metres per second; the resulting energy unit is kg·m²/s², which is a joule.

Worked unit check

For 1 gram, first convert to 0.001 kg. Using c ≈ 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s gives E ≈ 0.001 × (3.00 × 10⁸)² = 9.0 × 10¹³ J. The exponent matters: squaring 10⁸ produces 10¹⁶ before multiplying by the mass and the coefficient. This is the equivalent rest energy, not a prediction that a gram of ordinary material will release all of it in a practical process.

Rest energy is not the same as usable energy released

Physical reactions release only the energy difference between their initial and final states, subject to conservation laws and the details of the process. Chemical reactions typically involve energy changes far smaller than the complete rest-energy equivalent of the participating mass. Nuclear processes can convert a larger fraction of rest mass into other forms of energy, but the amount depends on the reaction and its products.

When a simple calculation is not enough

This calculator illustrates mass–energy equivalence; it does not model a nuclear reaction, radiation transport, conversion efficiency or engineering feasibility. For relativistic particles with motion, total energy also includes kinetic energy, and the full relativistic energy–momentum relationship may be required. Treat the result as a physics quantity with defined assumptions, not an estimate of practical energy recovery.

Mass–energy calculator FAQ

Why is the energy value so large for a small mass?

The speed of light is about 3 × 10⁸ m/s, and the formula squares that value. Even a small mass therefore gives a very large numerical rest-energy equivalent.

Does 1 gram of matter release 9 × 10¹³ joules in ordinary conditions?

No. That is the rest-energy equivalent under E = mc². Ordinary chemical or mechanical processes do not convert all the mass into usable energy.

Which units should I use?

Use kilograms for mass, metres per second for c and joules for energy. Convert grams to kilograms before calculating.

Does this formula include kinetic energy?

E = mc² represents rest energy. A moving relativistic object requires the broader energy–momentum relationship to account for motion.
