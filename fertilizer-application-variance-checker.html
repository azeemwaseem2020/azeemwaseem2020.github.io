Fertilizer Application Variance Checker

Compare a documented plan with the quantity actually applied. This record checker calculates product and labelled-nitrogen differences by application, helping you reconcile field notes and stock records. It does not decide whether an application rate is agronomically appropriate.

How the variance is calculated

Product variance (kg) = actual product applied − planned product. A positive result means more product was recorded than planned; a negative result means less product was recorded. Labelled nitrogen (kg) = product kilograms × nitrogen percentage ÷ 100. Nitrogen variance is the actual labelled nitrogen minus the planned labelled nitrogen. Percentage variance is product variance divided by planned product, multiplied by 100; it is not defined when the planned quantity is zero.

Example interpretation

If the plan records 100 kg of a 46% nitrogen product but the field record shows 95 kg applied, the product variance is −5 kg and the labelled-nitrogen variance is −2.3 kg N. This is a bookkeeping comparison, not proof that the crop is deficient or that more fertilizer should be applied. Reconcile weighing, field area, product grade, spills, remaining stock and the approved agronomic plan before making decisions.

Recordkeeping checklist

  • Record the field identifier, crop, date, operator and equipment separately in your farm log.
  • Use the net product weight actually applied; do not treat bag-equivalents as precise field measurements.
  • Check whether different products were used in the same application. This checker compares one nitrogen-labelled product per row.
  • Keep planned and actual records separate; never overwrite the original plan with actual figures.
  • Investigate large differences with a qualified local adviser and your original recommendation before changing future applications.

Limitations

This tool compares the entered quantities only. It does not account for phosphate or potash, nitrogen from other products, soil supply, manure, irrigation, nutrient losses, field-area errors or product distribution uniformity. Its result is not a fertilizer prescription, crop diagnosis or safety determination.
