How the calculator works

Monthly units are converted to average daily energy using 30 days. A first-pass PV estimate is daily kWh divided by peak sun hours and overall system efficiency. Panel count is rounded up from the selected panel wattage.

For appliances, daily energy is watts × hours ÷ 1,000. The appliance method also estimates connected load for inverter planning.

Solar battery sizing

A simple battery estimate is backup load in kW × backup hours ÷ usable depth of discharge. Voltage converts nominal battery energy to approximate Ah. Real battery selection must also consider chemistry, temperature, aging, round-trip efficiency and manufacturer limits.

Solar inverter sizing

Inverter sizing depends on simultaneous continuous load and starting surge. Motors, pumps, refrigerators and compressors can have high starting demand. PV input limits, MPPT range, battery compatibility and grid requirements must be checked against the actual inverter.

5kW solar example

A 5 kW target with 550 W modules gives 5,000 ÷ 550 = 9.09, so 10 panels would provide 5.5 kW of nameplate capacity. Actual energy production depends on site conditions and losses.

Common mistakes

  • Using bill amount instead of kWh when units are available.
  • Treating all daylight as peak-sun hours.
  • Selecting an inverter only from total panel watts.
  • Sizing batteries from the whole house when only selected loads need backup.
  • Ignoring shading, orientation, temperature and wiring losses.
  • Treating a calculator estimate as a final electrical design.

From electricity use to a practical solar estimate

A solar calculation should begin with energy demand rather than a panel count alone. Monthly electricity units can provide a useful starting point, while an appliance-by-appliance estimate can help identify daily load, peak demand and battery needs. Actual system design also depends on location, usable roof area, shading, orientation, equipment efficiency, backup expectations and local installation conditions.

  1. Estimate daily or monthly energy consumption.
  2. Identify appliances that may run at the same time to understand peak load.
  3. Choose a reasonable planning assumption for solar yield and system losses.
  4. Size the inverter around the required load and surge characteristics.
  5. Size battery storage separately according to the desired backup duration and usable capacity.
  6. Have final equipment selection checked against site and manufacturer specifications.

A practical solar load workflow: from appliance list to a safer first estimate

Use the calculator as a transparent first-pass model. Begin with the goal: reducing daytime grid use, supporting selected essential loads during outages, or operating independently from the grid. These goals are not interchangeable. A grid-connected system may prioritise daytime energy, while backup and off-grid systems must also consider storage, night-time demand, autonomy and how the system behaves during poor solar weather.

Step 1 — Separate power from energy

Record each appliance's rated running power in watts and a realistic daily operating time. Estimate daily energy as watts × hours ÷ 1,000 = kWh/day. For example, a 120 W fan used for 8 hours represents about 0.96 kWh/day. This is an estimate: thermostats, speed settings, compressor cycles and actual operating patterns can change consumption. Use a nameplate or manufacturer specification when possible, and avoid treating a device's maximum rating as its constant draw.

Step 2 — Check simultaneous and starting demand

Add the running watts of the appliances likely to operate at the same time to estimate continuous load. Separately identify motors, pumps, refrigerators and compressors that may draw a short starting surge. Do not assume the inverter's advertised peak rating can be sustained continuously; compare both continuous and surge specifications with the actual loads and the manufacturer's permitted duration.

Step 3 — Estimate PV capacity, then test the assumptions

A simplified first-pass estimate is PV array kW ≈ daily energy (kWh) ÷ [peak-sun-hours × overall performance factor]. If daily use is 6 kWh, peak sun hours are 5 and the assumed performance factor is 0.80, the arithmetic gives 6 ÷ (5 × 0.80) = 1.5 kW. This is an illustrative energy-balance estimate, not a guaranteed production forecast or final design. Seasonal sunlight, shade, panel temperature, orientation, inverter limits and the required reliability can change the result.

Step 4 — Size backup for the loads you actually need

Choose the essential loads first, then estimate their energy over the required backup period. A simplified battery-side energy estimate must account for usable depth of discharge and conversion losses; nominal battery capacity is not the same as energy delivered to appliances. Confirm battery chemistry, voltage, current limits, temperature range, inverter compatibility and manufacturer instructions before selecting equipment.

Data checklist before you trust the result

  • Use electricity-bill kWh rather than bill amount when available; tariffs and taxes make cost a poor proxy for energy.
  • Record seasonal use and whether appliances run together.
  • Identify surge loads and loads that must remain on during an outage.
  • Check roof shade, usable area, orientation, structural suitability and safe access.
  • Compare the estimate with equipment datasheets and obtain site-specific advice for wiring, protection, earthing, battery safety and applicable local rules.

Solar load calculator FAQs

Is a 5 kW inverter enough for a 5 kW solar array?

Not automatically. Check the inverter's AC output rating, PV input voltage and current limits, permitted DC-to-AC ratio, battery compatibility and simultaneous load. Panel nameplate capacity and inverter output are different specifications.

Can I calculate the system from my electricity bill amount?

Use the bill's measured kWh or units if possible. The amount due may include fixed charges, taxes, adjustments or tariff blocks, so it does not reliably show daily energy demand on its own.

Does this estimate confirm that my roof or wiring is safe?

No. Roof structure, mounting, cable sizing, protective devices, earthing, isolation, battery location and utility interconnection require a site-specific assessment by a qualified professional under applicable rules.
