How the calculator works

Monthly units are converted to average daily energy using 30 days. A first-pass PV estimate is daily kWh divided by peak sun hours and overall system efficiency. Panel count is rounded up from the selected panel wattage.

For appliances, daily energy is watts × hours ÷ 1,000. The appliance method also estimates connected load for inverter planning.

Solar battery sizing

A simple battery estimate is backup load in kW × backup hours ÷ usable depth of discharge. Voltage converts nominal battery energy to approximate Ah. Real battery selection must also consider chemistry, temperature, aging, round-trip efficiency and manufacturer limits.

Solar inverter sizing

Inverter sizing depends on simultaneous continuous load and starting surge. Motors, pumps, refrigerators and compressors can have high starting demand. PV input limits, MPPT range, battery compatibility and grid requirements must be checked against the actual inverter.

Common solar planning questions

This calculator is designed around common solar planning questions: solar calculator Pakistan, solar load calculator, solar system size, solar panel capacity, solar battery backup and solar inverter sizing. These terms describe different parts of the same planning process, so the result should be treated as a starting estimate rather than a promise of production or savings.

Which calculation should I use?

  • Use the load method when you know appliance wattage and expected running hours.
  • Use monthly units when you have reliable kWh consumption from your electricity bill.
  • Use the battery inputs when backup duration is important.
  • Use inverter planning to check continuous and starting loads separately.

5kW solar example

A 5 kW target with 550 W modules gives 5,000 ÷ 550 = 9.09, so 10 panels would provide 5.5 kW of nameplate capacity. Actual energy production depends on site conditions and losses.

Common mistakes

  • Using bill amount instead of kWh when units are available.
  • Treating all daylight as peak-sun hours.
  • Selecting an inverter only from total panel watts.
  • Sizing batteries from the whole house when only selected loads need backup.
  • Ignoring shading, orientation, temperature and wiring losses.
  • Treating a calculator estimate as a final electrical design.

From electricity use to a practical solar estimate

A solar calculation should begin with energy demand rather than a panel count alone. Monthly electricity units can provide a useful starting point, while an appliance-by-appliance estimate can help identify daily load, peak demand and battery needs. Actual system design also depends on location, usable roof area, shading, orientation, equipment efficiency, backup expectations and local installation conditions.

  1. Estimate daily or monthly energy consumption.
  2. Identify appliances that may run at the same time to understand peak load.
  3. Choose a reasonable planning assumption for solar yield and system losses.
  4. Size the inverter around the required load and surge characteristics.
  5. Size battery storage separately according to the desired backup duration and usable capacity.
  6. Have final equipment selection checked against site and manufacturer specifications.

Solar sizing: load, energy, battery and inverter are different calculations

A solar load calculation should not be confused with a complete solar system design. Appliance load describes instantaneous power, daily energy describes how much electricity is consumed over time, battery sizing describes stored energy, and inverter sizing describes the power that must be supplied at the required operating and surge conditions.

Start with appliance data

For each appliance, record its rated watts and expected hours of use. Daily energy is approximately watts × hours ÷ 1,000 for a single appliance in kWh. Add the appliance estimates to obtain a first-pass daily energy figure.

Why real systems need a margin

Actual solar production varies with sunlight, temperature, orientation, shading, panel performance, wiring losses and system efficiency. Battery performance also depends on chemistry, usable depth of discharge and temperature. A calculator estimate should therefore be treated as a planning starting point rather than a guaranteed production figure.

Worked example

If a 100 W appliance runs for 5 hours, its simple daily energy estimate is 0.5 kWh. If several appliances use 0.5, 1.2 and 2.0 kWh per day, their combined basic energy estimate is 3.7 kWh/day before system losses and other design assumptions.

Solar calculator FAQ

How many solar panels do I need?

Panel count depends on daily energy demand, panel wattage, available sunlight and system losses. Use the energy estimate as the starting input rather than choosing a panel count from appliance watts alone.

How do I size an inverter?

Inverter sizing depends on simultaneous running load, starting or surge demand and the inverter's continuous and peak ratings. Motor loads such as pumps and compressors can have higher starting demand.

How do I estimate battery backup?

Battery planning uses the required load, backup duration, battery voltage, usable capacity and efficiency assumptions. Do not equate battery nameplate capacity directly with usable delivered energy.

Is a solar calculator an installation design?

No. Electrical protection, wiring, local rules, roof conditions, structural limits, battery safety and equipment specifications require site-specific design and verification.

Load, daily energy and solar system sizing are different

A solar load calculation starts with the appliances or equipment you expect to operate. Three related figures should then be kept separate: connected load in watts or kilowatts, daily energy use in watt-hours or kilowatt-hours, and the solar system capacity selected to supply that energy. A large appliance can have a high running load but operate for only a short time; a smaller appliance can use more daily energy if it runs for many hours.

Running watts versus starting watts

Motors, pumps, refrigerators and air-conditioning equipment can draw a higher starting current than their normal running demand. That matters when checking inverter capacity and surge capability. Do not treat a starting surge as if it were continuous daily consumption unless the equipment specification says so.

Why panel size cannot be taken from load alone

Panel capacity is only one part of system design. Solar resource, usable sunlight, orientation, temperature, shading, wiring losses, inverter efficiency, battery losses and the amount of energy you want the system to cover all affect the final design. This calculator is therefore a planning aid rather than a stamped electrical design.

Off-grid, hybrid and grid-connected planning

An off-grid system normally needs energy storage and enough generation for the required operating pattern. A hybrid system may use solar, batteries and the grid together. A grid-connected system can have different backup requirements because the grid remains available under normal conditions. The same appliance list can therefore lead to different system choices.

Best workflow

List appliances, enter realistic running watts and hours, review daily energy, identify high-surge equipment, then compare the result with the inverter, battery and panel calculators. For installation, protection, wiring and code compliance, use a qualified solar or electrical professional and the equipment manufacturer's specifications.
