Sample-size planning

Sample size depends on the study design and assumptions. For a proportion estimate, a common approximation uses the expected proportion, confidence level and margin of error. For means, variability and the desired precision are also important. A calculator gives a mathematical planning result, not a guarantee of study quality.

Worked example

For a large population with an assumed proportion of 50%, 95% confidence and a 5 percentage-point margin of error, the familiar simple-random-sample approximation is about 385 observations before finite-population correction, nonresponse or design effects.

Research practice

Surveys in Pakistan may need adjustments for cluster sampling, stratification, nonresponse and the actual target population. If a study compares groups, sample size should be planned around the primary comparison rather than only an overall proportion.

FAQs

Does larger confidence require more observations? Usually yes. Does a smaller margin of error require more? Yes. Does sample size remove bias? No; a large biased sample can still be biased.

Sample Size: What the Estimate Means

Sample-size calculations estimate how many observations are needed to achieve a chosen precision or statistical power. For a proportion estimate, common inputs include confidence level, expected proportion and margin of error. For experiments, power and minimum detectable effect also matter. The correct formula depends on the study design.

For a simple proportion survey, the approximate formula is n = z²p(1−p)/E², where z is the critical value for the selected confidence level, p is the expected proportion and E is the desired margin of error. Using p = 0.5 is conservative when the true proportion is unknown because it produces the largest basic sample size for a given confidence and error.

For example, a survey using 95% confidence and a ±5 percentage-point margin of error with p = 0.5 gives a basic estimate of about 385 observations before finite-population or design adjustments. If the population is small, sampling without replacement can reduce the required number.

Real-world considerations

In Pakistan, a national survey may require stratification by province, urban/rural status or other groups. A mathematically adequate total sample can still be misleading if the sampling frame excludes important parts of the population. Nonresponse and clustering can also increase the effective sample size required.

FAQs

Does a larger sample always remove bias? No. A large biased sample can remain biased.

What does margin of error mean? It describes the desired sampling precision under the model and assumptions used.

Is 385 always required? No. That number comes from a specific proportion-survey setup.
