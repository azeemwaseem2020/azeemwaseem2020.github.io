IRR formula

IRR is the discount rate r that makes net present value equal to zero: 0 = Σ CFₜ/(1+r)ᵗ. Because the equation is solved numerically, some cash-flow patterns can produce multiple IRRs or no conventional solution.

Worked example

For an initial outflow of −10,000 followed by 6,000 and 6,000 in the next two periods, IRR is the rate that makes the discounted inflows equal the 10,000 initial investment.

How to use

  1. List cash flows in chronological order.
  2. Use negative values for investments/outflows and positive values for inflows.
  3. Calculate IRR and review whether the cash-flow pattern has a meaningful solution.

Interpret IRR in the right period

The calculator treats each entered line as one equal period. If your cash flows are annual, the result is an annual rate; if they are monthly, the result is a monthly rate and must be annualized separately. For a monthly IRR r, the effective annualized rate is (1 + r)12 − 1 when the periods and compounding assumptions fit that convention.

Why IRR can mislead

Some cash-flow patterns change sign more than once and can produce multiple IRRs or no real solution. IRR also assumes interim cash flows can be reinvested at the calculated rate for a comparable return interpretation. Compare IRR with net present value, the investment's risk, timing, and a realistic required return before making a decision.

Check the cash-flow order

Keep every cash flow in chronological order and use a consistent period length. Include additional capital calls, maintenance costs, fees or terminal costs when they are relevant to the investment. The result is an estimate based on the cash flows you enter, not a guaranteed investment return.
