Ohm's law calculation context

Ohm's law connects voltage, current and resistance through V = IR. The three quantities can be solved from any two known values, making this a core electrical-calculation relationship.

Ohm's law in practical circuits

Ohm's law relates voltage, current and resistance for an ohmic component under the relevant operating conditions. It is most useful when the component behaves approximately linearly.

Worked example

With 12 V across a 6 Ω resistor, the ideal current is 2 A. The same relationship can be rearranged when current or resistance is the unknown.

Where the simple model needs care

Incandescent lamps, diodes and many electronic components are not constant-resistance devices. Their operating point can change as voltage, current or temperature changes.

Ohm's law FAQ

Does Ohm's law apply to every component?

No. It is a model for ohmic behavior rather than a universal rule for every electrical device.

Checking an Ohm's law result

Keep voltage in volts, current in amperes and resistance in ohms. If you know voltage and resistance, calculate current with I = V/R. If you know current and resistance, calculate voltage with V = IR. If you know voltage and current, calculate resistance with R = V/I, provided current is non-zero.

Worked reverse example

A 24 V supply across a 12 Ω resistor gives I = 24/12 = 2 A. The resistor's ideal power dissipation is P = VI = 48 W, so the component would need an appropriate power rating; the calculation alone does not mean a small resistor can safely dissipate that power.

Practical limits

Use this relationship for components that behave approximately ohmically at the operating point. Real components can heat up, change resistance or require a non-linear model. Never probe mains circuits or energized equipment unless qualified and using appropriate safety procedures.

More Ohm's law questions

What if resistance is zero?

The ideal equation I = V/R is undefined at zero resistance. A real circuit's current is limited by the source, wiring and other components, so do not use the ideal formula to predict a safe short-circuit current.

Can this calculator find power?

Power can be derived from compatible values using P = VI, P = I²R or P = V²/R. Use the Electric Power Calculator for a focused power calculation.
