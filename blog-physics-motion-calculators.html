Which motion calculator do I need?

  • Speed: distance and elapsed time.
  • Velocity: displacement and time when direction matters.
  • Acceleration: change in velocity over time.
  • Kinematics: several constant-acceleration variables such as u, v, a, s and t.
  • Free fall: ideal vertical motion under gravity.
  • Projectile motion: two-dimensional ideal launch problems.

Unit and assumption checklist

Use consistent units, identify the known values, check the assumed gravitational acceleration, and decide whether the problem is one-dimensional or two-dimensional. Ideal models usually ignore air resistance and other real-world effects.

Frequently asked questions

What does this calculator help estimate?

It applies the stated inputs and standard formula to produce an estimate. The result should be checked against the equipment specification, local conditions and the assumptions used.

Are the results exact?

No calculation is exact when real-world inputs vary. Treat the result as a planning estimate and verify important decisions with current technical specifications.

Which inputs should I check first?

Check the units, rated values, system conditions and any losses or efficiency assumptions before relying on the final result.

Choose the equation from the known quantities

For constant acceleration, common kinematic relationships include v = u + at, s = ut + ½at² and v² = u² + 2as. The right equation depends on which variables are known and whether acceleration can reasonably be treated as constant.

Units first

Keep metres, seconds and metres per second consistent. If a problem gives km/h, convert speed before combining it with metre-based equations unless the equation has been rewritten for another unit system.

Free fall is a special case

Near Earth's surface, introductory problems often use approximately 9.8 m/s² for gravitational acceleration. The sign depends on the chosen coordinate direction; the important point is to use one sign convention consistently.

Projectile motion needs assumptions

Basic projectile equations usually assume negligible air resistance and a uniform gravitational field. Real trajectories can differ because of drag, wind, spin and changing atmospheric conditions.

How to validate a result

  1. Check units.
  2. Check the sign convention.
  3. Ask whether acceleration is constant.
  4. Test whether the magnitude is physically plausible.
  5. Substitute the result back into another applicable equation.

FAQ

When should I use a kinematics calculator?

Use one when the problem matches a constant-acceleration model and the known variables are compatible with the selected equation.

Why can two motion calculators give different answers?

They may use different units, signs, assumptions or definitions of the input variables.
