What this result really means

The result is a planning estimate, not a final installation design. Daily kWh describes energy demand; peak sun hours and efficiency translate that demand into an approximate PV size; panel wattage converts the size into a practical module count.

For a real project, confirm the roof, orientation, shading, module data, inverter limits, electrical protection, local rules and battery strategy with a qualified installer.

1. Why the calculator gives this number

PV capacity (kW) = daily energy demand (kWh) ÷ [peak sun hours × system efficiency]. The panel count is the calculated PV capacity in watts divided by the selected panel wattage, rounded up because a real installation uses whole modules.

Example: 15 kWh/day ÷ (5 × 0.80) = 3.75 kW. With 550 W panels, 3,750 ÷ 550 = 6.82, so the practical first-pass count is 7 panels, or 3.85 kW of nameplate capacity.

2. Assumptions behind the estimate

  • Daily energy use: the calculator assumes your kWh/day figure represents the energy the solar system needs to support.
  • Peak sun hours: this is a planning input, not the same thing as the number of daylight hours.
  • System efficiency: this is a combined planning allowance for losses. Real losses can vary with temperature, wiring, inverter conversion, dust/soiling, mismatch and other site conditions.
  • Panel rating: the entered wattage is the panel's nameplate rating under its rated test conditions; real output changes with operating conditions.

Professional PV models also account for site and system characteristics and explicitly warn that predictions contain assumptions and uncertainties.

3. Pakistan summer vs winter: what changes?

Do not assume that a 5 kW array will produce 5 kW all day or the same energy every month. Solar production changes with the available solar resource, weather, module temperature, orientation, shading and system losses. A good Pakistan estimate should therefore be treated as a planning range rather than a guaranteed monthly output.

  • Summer: long days can support strong energy production, but high module temperatures, dust, haze and other losses can affect actual output.
  • Winter: shorter days and changing solar conditions can reduce daily production in some locations, although cooler modules can have a beneficial effect on module performance.
  • Monsoon/cloudy periods: cloud cover can reduce solar irradiance and therefore daily generation.

For a location-specific production estimate, use local solar-resource/weather data and equipment specifications rather than one nationwide assumption. PVWatts, for example, uses location-based weather data and models system losses, while noting that predictions contain uncertainty.

4. Shading: why it matters

Shade from trees, buildings, walls, water tanks or nearby rows reduces the sunlight reaching the PV array. The effect is not safely represented by one universal percentage because the timing, shape and location of the shade matter.

Before installation, check the roof at different times of day and across seasons. A professional design can also model shading and string/module behaviour. PVWatts identifies shading and soiling as separate system-loss categories and notes that shading analysis tools can provide a more site-specific estimate.

5. How to check inverter sizing

Do not select an inverter only because its headline kW number looks similar to the panel array. Check the inverter datasheet for:

  • Maximum recommended PV array power
  • Maximum PV input voltage
  • MPPT operating voltage range
  • Maximum input current per MPPT
  • Number of MPPT trackers and string arrangement
  • Maximum AC output and the intended grid/load configuration

Then compare those limits with the selected panel's voltage/current specifications and the calculated array. Detailed PV modelling uses actual module and inverter specifications when available, rather than relying only on generic assumptions.

6. Do you need a battery?

Not always. A battery becomes more relevant when your priority is backup during outages, using stored solar energy after sunset, or increasing self-consumption. A grid-connected daytime-use system may have a different battery requirement from an off-grid or backup-focused system.

If you need backup, use the Solar Battery Calculator after estimating your PV system. Battery size should be based on the loads you actually want to run, backup duration, usable depth of discharge, battery efficiency and the battery/inverter specifications.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Using appliance wattage as if it were daily energy consumption.
  • Assuming rated panel wattage is continuous real-world output.
  • Ignoring shading, dust, temperature and wiring losses.
  • Choosing an inverter without checking MPPT voltage/current limits.
  • Adding panels without checking inverter, roof and electrical constraints.
  • Treating a planning estimate as a final electrical design.

FAQ

Can I use a different panel wattage?

Yes. Enter the exact rated wattage of the panel you are comparing and the calculator will recalculate the module count.

Should I simply add extra panels for winter?

Not automatically. Extra PV capacity can help in some designs, but it must be checked against inverter limits, roof space, string voltage/current, shading and the system's operating strategy.

Is this calculator enough to approve an installation?

No. It is a first-pass planning tool. Final system design should use site-specific solar resource, equipment datasheets, electrical calculations and applicable installation requirements.
