Frequently asked questions

What does Ohm’s law calculate?

Ohm’s law relates voltage, current and resistance for an applicable component or circuit model.

How do I calculate watts from volts and amps?

For a simple DC or resistive model, multiply voltage by current: P = VI.

Are basic electricity calculator results suitable for mains design?

No. Mains work can involve protection, power factor, reactive effects and applicable electrical codes. Use qualified professional practice for safety-critical work.

Ohm's law

V = IR

Voltage V equals current I multiplied by resistance R. Rearranging gives I = V/R and R = V/I. Use consistent units: volts, amperes and ohms.

Electrical power

P = VI = I²R = V²/R

These equivalent forms are useful when different quantities are known. For example, a 12 V load drawing 2 A consumes 24 W under a simple DC model.

Series resistance

For resistors in series, total resistance is the sum: Rtotal = R₁ + R₂ + … . Parallel networks use a different relationship, so do not apply the series formula to a parallel circuit.

DC versus AC

Basic Ohm's-law and power examples often assume resistive DC loads. AC systems can involve RMS values, power factor, reactive power and frequency-dependent impedance. A simple calculator should not be treated as a complete AC system model.

Safety boundary

Calculation is not a substitute for electrical design, protection selection or safe isolation procedures. Mains-voltage work should follow applicable codes and qualified professional practice.

Energy, power and time are different quantities

Power is the rate at which energy is used, measured in watts or kilowatts. Energy is accumulated use over time, commonly measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh). For a steady load, energy in kWh equals power in kW multiplied by hours of operation. A 1.5 kW appliance running for 2 hours uses 3 kWh under that simplified assumption.

Estimate cost from energy use

Multiply the energy estimate by the applicable per-kWh rate to get a basic energy charge. An actual bill may also include fixed charges, taxes, adjustments, slab pricing or other line items, so a simple estimate should not be presented as the final bill total. Use the rate and billing details from the relevant utility bill.

Why actual usage can differ

Appliances cycle on and off, and their rated power may differ from average power during use. Standby use, operating temperature, user settings and the number of hours switched on all affect consumption. Treat a nameplate-based estimate as a planning approximation and use a suitable meter when measured consumption is needed.

Convert power into energy and cost

A 1.5 kW appliance running for 2 hours uses 3 kWh of energy. At a hypothetical rate of PKR 60 per kWh, the energy charge would be PKR 180 before fixed charges, taxes or slab-based pricing. This example separates power (the rate of energy use) from energy (the amount consumed) and is not a current electricity tariff quote.

For a household bill, use the tariff and billing treatment that actually applies to the connection, including any time-of-use rate or adjustment. Do not multiply watts directly by a price per kWh without first converting power and duration into energy.
