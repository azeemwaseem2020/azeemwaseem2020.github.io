Which calculator should you use?

Start with the question you are trying to answer. A household bill estimate and an appliance running-cost estimate use different inputs, so choosing the matching tool helps avoid misleading results.

  • Your total monthly electric bill: use the US Electricity Bill Calculator when your plan can be approximated with one blended rate. Add fixed fees and other charges only when they are not already included in your rate.
  • Your utility has usage blocks: use the Tiered Electricity Rate Calculator when each rate applies only to the kWh inside a specific block. It models block-based tiers, not peak/off-peak time-of-use schedules.
  • You want to know what an AC costs to run: use the Air Conditioner Electricity Cost Calculator with the unit's electrical input watts where available, or an explicitly labeled BTU/h and EER estimate. Add your own electricity price.
  • You are exploring solar: start with the Solar Load Calculator to estimate energy demand. A load estimate alone does not determine system price, payback, export-credit value or guaranteed savings.

How to get a more realistic estimate

  1. Use the billing period's actual kWh when possible, and note the number of days covered.
  2. Read the rate labels carefully. Supply, delivery, fixed customer charges, taxes and credits may be listed separately.
  3. Check whether your plan uses a flat, tiered, time-of-use or demand-based tariff. Do not force a complex tariff into a simple model without noting the limitation.
  4. For appliances, prefer measured consumption or electrical input watts from the equipment label over cooling capacity alone.
  5. Compare the result with your current bill or utility tariff. Utility prices and rate rules vary by provider, plan, season and location.

Example: energy charge versus full bill

If a household uses 900 kWh and the applicable single energy rate is 16 cents per kWh, the energy charge is 900 × $0.16 = $144. If there is also a $15 fixed charge and $4 in additional charges not already included in the rate, the estimated total is $163. This is a worked example only; it is not a statement of a current average US price.

Useful reference and limitations

The US Energy Information Administration publishes state and national electricity price statistics for broad comparisons. Those averages are historical or aggregated context, not a quote for an individual utility account. See the EIA Electric Power Annual state price data and confirm current prices with your utility's published tariff.

Calcora estimates are only as complete as the inputs and pricing model. They do not automatically identify your utility, retrieve your account tariff, forecast future prices, or guarantee savings. For a bill that includes complex time-of-use pricing, demand charges, solar export credits or special adjustments, consult the utility's rate schedule and treat a simplified estimate as a starting point.
