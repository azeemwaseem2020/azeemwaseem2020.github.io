Voltage Drop Calculator

Estimate voltage lost across a circuit when its current and total resistance are known. This tool uses the simple resistive model; it does not replace a full cable-sizing calculation.

Voltage drop formula

For a known total circuit resistance, the resistive voltage drop is Vdrop = I × R, where current I is measured in amperes and resistance R in ohms. The result is in volts. Use the resistance of the complete current path, including the return conductor where relevant.

Worked example: 10 A through 0.5 Ω

For I = 10 A and R = 0.5 Ω, Vdrop = 10 × 0.5 = 5 V. On a 230 V supply, the estimated voltage remaining at the load is 230 − 5 = 225 V. The percentage drop is (5 ÷ 230) × 100 = 2.17%.

How to use the calculator

  1. Enter the circuit current in amperes.
  2. Enter the total resistance in ohms. Do not enter cable length here; this field expects resistance.
  3. Enter the supply voltage if you also want the estimated remaining voltage and percentage drop.
  4. Choose Calculate Voltage Drop and review the result against the requirements for your particular installation.

What the result does and does not mean

This calculation estimates the voltage drop caused by resistance. It assumes the entered current and resistance describe the circuit operating condition. It does not automatically account for conductor temperature, reactance, power factor, phase arrangement, harmonics, transient loads or changes in current.

Why cable length and conductor size matter

Longer conductors generally have more resistance, while a larger conductor cross-section generally has less resistance. A real cable calculation may also need copper or aluminium conductor data, one-way route length, return path, operating temperature, AC or DC supply, single- or three-phase configuration, load current and power factor. For AC circuits, reactance and phase angle can matter too.

For safe electrical design, follow applicable local codes and equipment requirements and ask a qualified electrician or engineer to verify the installation. Do not use a generic percentage as a universal legal limit: acceptable drop depends on the circuit purpose, jurisdiction and applicable standard.

Common causes of excessive voltage drop

  • Current higher than the circuit was designed to carry.
  • Long cable runs or conductors with insufficient cross-sectional area.
  • Loose, corroded or damaged connections that add resistance.
  • Undersized extension leads or unsuitable wiring arrangements.

Frequently asked questions

Does higher current increase voltage drop?

Yes. With resistance held constant, doubling the current doubles the resistive voltage drop.

Is voltage drop always a fault?

No. Some drop is expected in real conductors. It becomes a concern when it exceeds the limit appropriate to the equipment, circuit and applicable standards.

Does the calculator know my cable length?

No. It uses the total resistance you enter. To estimate resistance from cable length, conductor material and cross-sectional area, use a cable-specific calculation with the correct circuit arrangement and operating assumptions.

Can remaining voltage be negative?

The calculator reports the arithmetic result from your inputs. A negative result signals that the chosen current and resistance are inconsistent with a simple passive load supplied at that voltage; reassess the inputs and circuit model.
