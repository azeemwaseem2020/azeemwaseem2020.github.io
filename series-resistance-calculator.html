Resistors connected in series

In a series circuit, the same current passes through each resistor and the total resistance is the sum of the individual resistances. This is one of the simplest resistance models, but it should not be applied to parallel branches. The total voltage is distributed across the resistors according to their resistance values.

Formula

Rₜ = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + … . If a voltage V is applied to the series combination, Ohm's law gives I = V ÷ Rₜ. The voltage across resistor Rᵢ is Vᵢ = I × Rᵢ.

Worked example

For 4 Ω, 6 Ω and 10 Ω resistors in series, Rₜ = 20 Ω. With a 12 V source, current is 12 ÷ 20 = 0.6 A. The voltage drops are 2.4 V, 3.6 V and 6.0 V, which add back to 12 V.

Pakistan-specific example

A student assembling a low-voltage electronics experiment in a Pakistani lab can use the calculator to check the expected total resistance before powering the circuit. The actual circuit should still respect resistor power ratings, supply limits and safe laboratory practice.

Common questions

Is series resistance always larger? The total is the sum of positive resistances, so it exceeds each individual resistance.

Does current change between series resistors? In the ideal series model, the same current flows through each.

How is parallel different? Parallel branches share voltage and use reciprocal resistance relationships.

Does this include wire resistance? Only if you enter it as part of the resistance model.
